A BSTRACT
Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a critical phase in the post-training
pipeline of large language models (LLMs). As the frontier of LLM capabilities shifts from static reasoning to agentic tasks—characterized by external tool
invocation, multi-turn interactions, and long-horizon decision-making—existing
mainstream RL frameworks have grown increasingly unwieldy, raising the overhead of adoption, usage, and debugging. To address these challenges, we present
LoongSage, an agent-native, fully asynchronous RL framework with a lightweight
core of roughly 14K lines of code. Built upon Megatron for distributed training
and SGLang for high-throughput inference, LoongSage centralizes orchestration
within a single controller process and encapsulates all GPU-resident components
as Ray actors. It introduces AgentFlow, a unified execution paradigm that orchestrates both white-box and black-box agents. For asynchronous execution, it
integrates sliding-window strategies that bound trajectory staleness, and employs
TransferMesh, a topology-aware synchronization mechanism that routes weights
after each step over the fastest transport available to each receiver. Beyond agentic
RL, LoongSage incorporates a memory-efficient Teacher Manager that decouples
the scaling of teacher models from GPU memory constraints, natively supporting
Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation. Extensive evaluations across four complete
training runs, spanning complex reinforcement learning and multi-teacher distillation, demonstrate that task rewards improve steadily while the train–inference gap
is kept under continuous control, and the fully asynchronous pipeline removes
synchronous idle bubbles, sustaining hardware utilization.

I NTRODUCTION

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a pivotal post-training mechanism driving recent capability gains in large language models (LLMs). Historically, the GPT series leveraged Reinforcement
Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to translate human preferences into optimizable objectives,
establishing the viability of instruction-following assistants (Ouyang et al., 2022). More recently,
models like DeepSeek-R1 have demonstrated that, given verifiable rewards, complex behaviors such
as extended chain-of-thought reasoning, self-correction, and backtracking can emerge through RL
optimization (Guo et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2022). As the focus transitions from instruction following to complex reasoning, RL has shifted from a secondary fine-tuning step to a central phase for
instilling core model capabilities.
Despite these reasoning advancements, text generation alone is insufficient for executing complex,
real-world projects. This limitation has catalyzed exploration into tool-calling capabilities (Yao
et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023), driving the paradigm shift towards agentic RL. In this context,
reward signals are no longer confined to the correctness of single-turn responses. Instead, they evaluate an agent’s holistic ability to complete tasks by invoking tools, manipulating files, executing
tests, and refining actions iteratively based on environmental feedback. Consequently, coding, research, and computer-use agents have established end-to-end task completion as a new capability
frontier (Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). Compared to single-turn tasks,
agentic RL unfolds over extended horizons with persistent multi-turn states, necessitates sandboxed

Luo et al., 2025). This fundamental shift redefines the role of an RL framework from merely generating and scoring text to continuously driving and recording a system operating within dynamic

However, many existing RL frameworks are rooted in assumptions inherited from single-turn RLHF
or verifiable-reward workloads (Hu et al., 2025), rendering them suboptimal for agentic training.
While some systems introduce controller-centered abstractions (Sheng et al., 2025) or deliberately
narrow the pairing of backend engines (Zhu et al., 2025), none are architected natively for the complexities of agentic training, which directly translate into production overheads. For instance, memory constraints typically bottleneck the number of teachers in multi-teacher distillation, and synchronous train-inference loops suffer severe utilization drops due to long-tailed trajectory rollouts.
Furthermore, several systemic discrepancies manifest as implicit off-policy drift: mismatches in tokenization, numerical precision, and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing between the training and
inference engines. These are compounded by loose trajectory reconstruction—a subtle but accumulating divergence between the inference-generated sequences and the training-consumed data.
Because algorithmic hyperparameters cannot tune away these systemic issues, a modern framework
must elevate resource management, train-inference decoupling, numerical consistency, and trajectory fidelity to first-class design primitives.
To address these critical gaps, we present LoongSage, a lightweight agentic RL training framework
engineered for production-grade workloads. Built specifically upon Megatron (Shoeybi et al., 2019)
for distributed training and SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024) for high-throughput inference, LoongSage
encapsulates the most demanding and error-prone complexities of agentic RL within its architecture
while maintaining a lightweight, extensible, and user-friendly core. Notably, we open-source not
only the framework but also the validated training recipes, enabling researchers to initialize runs
from configurations proven on frontier models rather than starting from scratch. Its key features
include:
• Seamless Integration of Any Agent: LoongSage accommodates any white-box or blackbox agent via a standard chat-completions interface with zero intrusion into agent logic. Its
AgentFlow paradigm maintains strict token-level alignment (Token-In, Token-Out; TITO),
feeding the training engine the exact token streams generated during inference, thereby
decoupling the framework core from domain-specific agent implementations.
• Staleness-Bounded Fully Asynchronous Training: By decoupling the sampling and optimization phases via a train-rollout buffer, the fully asynchronous pipeline substantially
improves hardware utilization. To strictly bound the resulting trajectory staleness, it employs sliding-window strategies, bringing off-policy drift under explicit control.
• Topology-Aware Weight Synchronization: Through TransferMesh, LoongSage transfers
weights from training to inference as a continuous named tensor stream. The system automatically selects the optimal transport layer—such as a CUDA IPC handle for colocated
GPUs or an NCCL broadcast for distributed nodes—based on the physical device topology,
keeping the synchronization process completely transparent to both backend engines.
• Full-Vocabulary Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation: An independent Teacher Manager treats teacher models as dynamically managed compute resources, decoupling the
permissible number of teachers from the student model’s GPU memory constraints to
enable scalable multi-teacher distillation. Furthermore, it natively supports a composable, multi-paradigm objective function—spanning PG-style, GKD-style, and hybrid distillation modes—equipped with built-in estimators including sampled-token, Top-K, fullvocabulary, and Jensen–Shannon Divergence (JSD) KL strategies.
• Systemic Train–Inference Consistency: To prevent off-policy drift, LoongSage treats
train–inference consistency as a systematically enforced property. It deploys a defense-indepth architecture combining Deterministic Source Alignment (e.g., MoE routing replay),
selective precision pinning for critical layers, and algorithmic residual corrections (including TIS, M2PO, and OPSM; detailed in Section 4.5) to guarantee the optimization plane
reliably tracks the sampling distribution.
• Lightweight, Usable, and Highly Extensible: By maintaining a strict zero-fork dependency on its backend engines, the lightweight core transparently inherits upstream opti2

2.1

T HE E VOLUTION OF LLM P OST-T RAINING

Compositional Paradigms. Modern LLM post-training is converging toward a compositional
pipeline rather than a single optimization objective. Supervised fine-tuning establishes base capabilities and response formats, after which reinforcement learning optimizes the policy against a
reward signal. Building on knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), on-policy distillation then
adds a denser supervision channel, transferring the output distributions of one or more teachers onto
the student’s own rollouts (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). These stages differ in where
supervision originates and in whether generation happens inside the training loop, yet they increasingly share the same training and inference infrastructure. Frameworks therefore need to compose
objectives and data flows without forcing every new method into a separate execution stack.
From RLHF to RLVR. Reinforcement learning entered LLM post-training as RLHF, which fits
a reward model to preference comparisons and optimizes the policy with PPO (Schulman et al.,
2017; Ouyang et al., 2022). Later work then moved in two directions, one of which removes online
RL altogether by reparameterizing the preference objective, as DPO does (Rafailov et al., 2023).
The other retains online RL but replaces the learned reward model with a programmatic checker,
which in turn simplifies the algorithms themselves. GRPO drops the value network in favor of
group-relative advantages (Shao et al., 2024), and DAPO combines decoupled clipping, dynamic
sampling, and token-level loss aggregation for large-scale RLVR (Yu et al., 2026). The transition
also moves the systems cost from reward-model and reference-policy management toward rollout
generation, program execution, sample filtering, and reliable aggregation of completed outcomes.
Agentic RL. As models are deployed in more complex settings, RL is shifting toward agentic tasks
with multi-turn interaction and external environments, building on early evidence that LLMs can be
taught to invoke tools and APIs, whether by in-context prompting or by fine-tuning (Yao et al., 2023;
Schick et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024). An agentic reward evaluates whether a task was completed
rather than whether a single response was correct, so producing it depends on tool use and sandboxed
execution against a live environment (Yang et al., 2024; Jimenez et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). Unlike
single-turn generation, the resulting trajectory contains intermediate observations, actions, and tool
results whose contribution to the final outcome may be delayed. A practical training system must
therefore preserve the generated sequence and its metadata across multiple turns, tolerate heavytailed episode lengths and external-environment failures, and manage the sandbox lifecycle (Luo
et al., 2025).
2.2

D ISTRIBUTED T RAINING AND I NFERENCE S YSTEMS

Parallelism and Distributed Training. Large-model training combines data, tensor, pipeline, and
expert parallelism to overcome single-GPU limits, and Megatron-LM provides optimized implementations of these strategies (Shoeybi et al., 2019). DeepSpeed and ZeRO instead reduce memory use
by partitioning optimizer states, gradients, and parameters (Rasley et al., 2020; Rajbhandari et al.,
2020). On top of these placement decisions, RL post-training adds a further requirement, because
the inference side must generate rollouts concurrently or alternately with the distributed training
side. Resource placement and the synchronization relation between the two sides therefore become
part of the framework design.
Orchestration and Inference Engines. Ray manages heterogeneous pipeline components across
a cluster (Moritz et al., 2018), while rollout generation relies on dedicated serving engines. vLLM
improves throughput through continuous batching and PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023), and
SGLang targets multi-turn and structured LLM programs (Zheng et al., 2024). Because rollout

requests have variable lengths and may invoke tools whereas training requires tightly synchronized
collective computation, orchestration must coordinate two distinct lifecycles and resource layouts
rather than treating inference as a simple preprocessing step.
2.3

RL F RAMEWORKS

Foundational RL Frameworks. OpenRLHF (Hu et al., 2025) established an accessible combination of distributed training and high-throughput inference. NeMo-Aligner (Shen et al., 2024) targets
Megatron-based scale, TRL (von Werra et al., 2020) emphasizes Hugging Face usability, verl (Sheng
et al., 2025) uses a controller-centered HybridFlow model, and slime (Zhu et al., 2025) narrows the
dependency surface by pairing Megatron-LM with SGLang. Together these systems span accessibility, ecosystem integration, large-scale backend support, expressive control flow, and a deliberately
small dependency surface. Agentic workloads, however, further require explicit support for trajectory structure, resource sharing, and the boundary between rollout and optimization.
Asynchronous Execution and Rollout Management. AReaL (Fu et al., 2026) makes asynchronous generation and training the default and mitigates long-tail latency by buffering unfinished
trajectories for later continuation, which improves utilization at the cost of trajectory staleness.
Agent-Lightning (Luo et al., 2025) instead disaggregates the agent from the trainer, while agent
frameworks and benchmarks such as SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024), OpenHands (Wang et al.,
2025), WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024), and OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) motivate support for longhorizon interaction and persistent state. These directions expose a central trade-off in which decoupling improves utilization and accommodates long-running interactions while also increasing
coordination complexity and the possibility that training consumes data produced by older policy
versions. A framework should therefore expose this trade-off as a configurable policy rather than a
fixed behavior, and keep one interface for trajectories, weight transfer, and training regardless of the
execution mode.

T HE L OONG S AGE F RAMEWORK

This section describes the system architecture of LoongSage and the orchestration of its principal components within an agentic RL training pipeline. By establishing strict boundaries across
orchestration, data representation, and backend integration, the framework accommodates both
synchronous and fully asynchronous execution within a unified architecture. Figure 1 situates
LoongSage against the most closely related systems across four structural axes: training and inference backends, agent integration methodology, support for multi-teacher distillation, and execution asynchrony. Rather than pursuing broad backend agnosticism or unrestricted scheduling,
LoongSage commits to an optimized pairing of specific engines and bounded asynchronous execution. This focus lets the framework address the central challenges of agentic workloads: ensuring
the fidelity of trajectories fed to the optimizer, orchestrating multi-teacher distillation at scale, and
maintaining observability over the train–inference gap (detailed in Section 4.5).
3.1

D ESIGN T RADE - OFFS

The architectural design of LoongSage is guided by three core principles, aimed at ensuring that
long-running agentic RL training remains transparent, deterministic, and diagnosable in the event of
systemic failures.
One controller, many workers. All system orchestration is centralized within a single controller
process, where the Trainer acts as the sole state machine dictating every phase transition. GPUresident components operate as Ray actors, exposing coarse-grained control methods and returning
ObjectRefs. Crucially, the controller executes no forward passes and holds no model parameters,
guaranteeing that the global state of a long-running distributed job can be fully inspected from
this single process. Control signals are kept intentionally coarse to amortize the overhead of Ray
serialization and remote procedure calls (RPCs).
Token-level internal boundaries. The internal interface between the sampling and optimization
planes operates strictly at the token level—exchanging token sequences coupled with their respective
log-probabilities, loss masks, and weight versions. Human-readable text exists only at the outermost

3.2

S YSTEM O RGANIZATION

LoongSage structurally decomposes the RL pipeline into seven interacting planes: control, sampling, training, algorithm, data, transfer, and resource. These planes communicate exclusively via
well-defined data objects and control interfaces, preserving the readability of the core logic while
allowing targeted functionality to be added through configuration.
The control plane is anchored by the Trainer, which orchestrates the state transitions for each
algorithmic step. Dedicated lifecycle managers—TrainManager, RolloutManager, and
TeacherManager—oversee the training, inference, and teacher worker pools, respectively. The
RolloutSampler maps step-level data requirements into batched sampling tasks. To prevent
tangled dependencies, these managers never invoke one another directly. Instead, the Trainer
sequences them according to the current training phase, cleanly isolating orchestration logic from
the compute backends and enabling independent backend evolution.
As illustrated in Figure 2, the sampling plane is governed by AgentFlow, which translates unstructured multi-turn agent interactions into trainable trajectories. By operating the agent externally,
LoongSage embraces the disaggregated design philosophy advocated by Agent-Lightning. AgentFlow’s token-level alignment and multi-agent support are detailed in Section 4.1. The training
plane wraps the Megatron-based worker pool that executes the forward, backward, and optimizer
passes over admitted trajectories. The algorithm plane exposes registries for advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2015), policy losses, and KL divergence constraints, and additionally provides configurable importance-sampling corrections (Zheng et al., 2025) and off-policy sequence
masking (DeepSeek-AI, 2025).
The data plane governs Multi-Data-Source Mixed Training, managing stateful data loading, admission filtering, and data-parallel partitioning. It enables the independent configuration of agents,
rewards, and sampling parameters per source, while strictly maintaining global data mixture propor5

3.3

T RAINING F LOW AND P LACEMENT

Under the default synchronous execution mode, a training step proceeds through four distinct
stages. During the sampling stage, the RolloutSampler fetches prompt groups from various
data sources and dispatches sampling tasks. In the admission stage, completed trajectories are
grouped and evaluated by a data filter. Because rejections occur post-generation, the sampler proactively over-samples a configurable buffer per data source, refilling rejected slots within a bounded
budget and enforcing step-level timeouts for stalled requests. During the aggregation stage, the
controller aligns admitted trajectories with the active policy version and data-parallel layout, while
teacher workers compute necessary distillation signals. Finally, in the update stage, training workers execute the forward, backward, and optimizer passes, concluding with the transfer of updated
parameters back to the inference engine for the subsequent rollout.
The fully asynchronous mode eliminates this strict synchronization barrier, enabling the sampling
and optimization engines to make concurrent progress via a decoupled train–rollout buffer. The
buffering, freshness constraints, and recovery mechanics of this mode are detailed in Section 4.2.
Execution coupling and resource placement are treated as orthogonal architectural axes (Figure 3).
Colocated placement time-multiplexes device memory so that both engines and the weight-update
phase fit on the same GPUs, whereas disaggregated placement dedicates disjoint hardware pools to
each engine. Both placement strategies share an identical underlying scheduler, controller, AgentFlow paradigm, and weight-transfer interface, allowing deployments to scale flexibly based on
model size and cluster topology. Among the four possible combinations of these two axes, three
are structurally supported. Fully asynchronous execution inherently conflicts with colocation, as
overlapping rollout and training mandates simultaneous memory residency for both engines.

Token-In, Token-Out (TITO). A core mechanism of AgentFlow is storing the exact tokens consumed and generated by the inference engine, deliberately avoiding the practice of re-tokenizing
textual messages prior to training. Such re-tokenization often introduces compounding discrepancies between the sampled and trained sequences due to whitespace handling, special tokens, reasoning markers, or tool-call serialization. Under TITO, high-level textual requests and responses are
mapped transparently to their internal token representations at the framework’s outermost boundary,
ensuring the sampled and trained sequences remain identical. Consequently, completed trajectories can be annotated with validity, scalar, or fine-grained rewards without corrupting the original
sampling record.
For white-box agents, AgentFlow reuses established token prefixes as conversational histories expand. For black-box agents, it dynamically reconstructs mutated context segments while preserving
their topological relationships. Should a long-horizon trajectory be interrupted, its sandbox state
can also be preserved and reused, preventing the loss of intermediate file edits or process states.
The framework’s sandbox interface provides native Kubernetes and Docker backends. Thus, agents
requiring shell execution are securely isolated within ephemeral pods or containers whose lifecycles
are strictly bound to the trajectory.
Compaction and Multi-Agent Control. State-of-the-art black-box agents (e.g., OpenCode, Claude
Code, and Codex) natively embed automatic context compaction and multi-agent collaboration
mechanisms. These behaviors inherently rewrite or fork the conversation history, violating the standard linear Markov Decision Process (MDP) assumption. Consequently, a naive linear trajectory
store would immediately fail prefix matching upon context compaction or subtask delegation. To
overcome this, AgentFlow records every trajectory as a directed tree of segments (Figure 6). Tree
edges are defined by segment id and parent segment id pointers, while an origin tag
explicitly categorizes each node as a mainline start (root), a compaction-induced continuation
(compact), or a spawned subtask (subagent).
Crucially, only mainline segments are designated as trainable, earning the right to populate the flat
token, loss-mask, and log-probability arrays depicted in Figure 5. If a prefix mismatch occurs and
the request is not explicitly a collab spawn, the trajectory opens a new mainline branch to retokenize the compacted context. Conversely, a collab spawn request forks a subagent segment

Bounding Staleness. In a fully asynchronous pipeline, maximizing hardware utilization inherently
trades off data freshness. If the inference engine significantly outpaces the trainer, the buffer accumulates trajectories generated by outdated policy weights, severely exacerbating off-policy drift.

and collection gates, strictly bounding the maximum allowable version gap between the generation
policy and the optimization policy. Furthermore, because LoongSage attributes metrics exclusively
to the data actually consumed during an optimization step, the systemic impact of each policy on
off-policy divergence remains fully transparent and directly observable in the training logs.

no-window (Figure 9a) dispatches a new group the moment an active slot frees up. Its only capacity limit exists to prevent host memory exhaustion rather than to bound off-policy drift. Because
a completed group immediately yields its slot, short requests finish and enter the training phase in
rapid succession. Meanwhile, a computationally heavy, long-horizon request may be interrupted by
multiple asynchronous weight updates without bound. This inherently introduces a severe lengthbiased sampling pathology, skewing the training distribution toward shorter trajectories.
window-gated (Figure 9b) imposes a hard gate, refusing to dispatch any task whose sequence
number exceeds the oldest in-flight one by more than the window W = B(1 + S). The window
advances only when the oldest group completes. While this strictly bounds staleness to ≤ S steps,
it risks severe pipeline under-utilization due to head-of-line blocking from long-tail requests. The
worst-case scenario occurs when the entire training pipeline stalls, waiting idly for a single straggler
to clear the window. In practice, however, the window typically advances fluidly as subsequent
requests finish ahead of the straggler.
windowed-fifo (Figure 9c) adopts its nomenclature and collection heuristic from Forge. It retains the aggressive capacity-based dispatch rule without generation bounds but restricts collection:
a completed group is only ingested if it falls within one batch of the oldest in-flight sequence number. Groups completing too far ahead are discarded and never trained. This bounds off-policy drift
once uncollected groups accumulate and critically enforces an ordering constraint that mitigates the
aforementioned short-trajectory bias (quantified against no-window in Section 5.3). The collection window is fixed at one batch. A smaller window would artificially constrain ordering within
a single step, while a significantly larger one would simply degenerate into no-window. The underlying buffer operates as a priority queue keyed by dataset order, ensuring the training sequence
mirrors synchronous mode as closely as physical completion times permit.
The net benefit of asynchronous execution hinges on the relative durations of the rollout and training
phases, and the ratio by which the GPU cluster is partitioned between them. Let x > 1 denote the
reciprocal of the cluster fraction dedicated to inference. Here R0 and T0 are the colocated wholecluster baseline times for rollout and training, E0 is the constant per-step overhead, and f captures
the super-linear efficiency of higher per-replica concurrency in a smaller inference pool. Under this
parameterization, training time scales as T0 x/(x − 1) and rollout time as R0 f (x). Because the two
phases overlap, a step’s latency is bounded by the slower of the two, yielding the analytical allocation
objective: minx>1 max(R0 f (x), T0 x/(x − 1) + E0 ). The exact scaling behavior of f is highly
workload-dependent. Section 5.3 empirically maps this function for a target workload, identifying
the inflection point beyond which scaling inference concurrency yields diminishing returns.

At its core, TransferMesh abstracts a global weight update as a named tensor stream. When the
sender yields parameters, the transfer layer coalesces them into contiguous memory buckets, recording metadata—tensor name, shape, data type, and byte offsets—into a separate layout descriptor.
Receivers fetch this lightweight descriptor first, allowing them to reconstruct the tensor stream for
the inference engine. This decouples the two engines’ internal memory layouts and amortizes the
high latency and kernel-launch overheads of point-to-point transfers of fragmented, fine-grained
tensors.
The underlying transport dynamically adapts to the discovered hardware topology. When training and inference processes are colocated on the same physical device, TransferMesh exchanges a
CUDA Inter-Process Communication (IPC) handle pointing to the sender’s memory bucket. This
achieves zero-copy parameter sharing, bypassing redundant VRAM allocations and PCIe/NVLink
traversals. For remote receivers, the bulk payload is broadcast via an optimized NCCL collective.
All metadata and IPC handles are transmitted out-of-band via a lightweight Gloo channel, letting re12

F ULL -VOCABULARY M ULTI -T EACHER O N -P OLICY D ISTILLATION

On-policy distillation trains a student model on its self-generated rollouts while leveraging one or
more teacher models for dense, token-level distributional supervision. LoongSage abstracts this
process as a highly composable, multi-paradigm objective deeply integrated into the primary training
loop, allowing it to function standalone or seamlessly combined with algorithms like GRPO or
GSPO.
The teacher signal provides two distinct supervision pathways: a policy-gradient regularizer (via a
KL penalty on the advantage) and a direct supervised loss interpolated with the RL objective:
Ât = At − λpg DKL (πstudent ∥ πteacher )t ,

By independently modulating these two pathways—the KL penalty (λpg ) and the supervised distillation loss (λgkd )—the unified loop natively supports PG-style regularization, GKD-style supervised
distillation, and hybrid settings. The KL divergence estimator is equally flexible, accommodating
sampled-token signals, Top-K truncation, full-vocabulary alignments, and Jensen–Shannon variants
based on the available teacher metadata.
Teacher Manager and Resource Multiplexing. Within the multi-teacher paradigm, the Teacher
Manager treats teacher models as dynamically scheduled compute resources rather than black-box
external API services. Under constrained device memory, multiple teachers are multiplexed onto a
single worker group, and inactive teacher weights are offloaded to host CPU memory. Consequently,
the permissible number of teachers is bounded by abundant host memory rather than scarce GPU
VRAM. The scheduler further guarantees that each teacher is swapped into device memory at most
once per forward pass, minimizing PCIe bandwidth thrashing.
Building on the per-source data plane (Section 3), each data source additionally binds an assigned
teacher. Because that plane holds the global data mixture invariant across ranks and mini-batches,
the data-mixing ratio maps directly onto each teacher’s relative gradient contribution. This architectural choice lets operators steer teacher dominance through data-routing configurations rather
than hand-tuned loss-weighting heuristics. Figure 11 illustrates this resource pooling arrangement.
Finally, teacher forward passes execute on the same Megatron backend as the student; this structural symmetry prevents inference-engine numerical discrepancies from corrupting the reverse-KL
objective.
Memory-Efficient Full-Vocabulary Distillation. To avoid the activation-memory bottleneck typically associated with massive vocabularies, LoongSage eschews transferring full probability vectors
across nodes. Instead, it transmits compact teacher hidden states—an optimization it shares with
DeepSeek-V4 (DeepSeek-AI, 2026)—but diverges significantly in its logit materialization strategy.
Rather than materializing the full-vocabulary logit tensor for the entire batch, LoongSage shards
the teacher’s output head using the student’s tensor-parallel (TP) layout and reconstructs the required distribution strictly at micro-batch granularity. The reconstructed logits are consumed onthe-fly by the loss function and immediately freed before the next micro-batch begins. This microbatch-scoped lifecycle keeps the memory footprint bounded, delivering the dense supervision of
full-vocabulary distillation without triggering OOM errors. In the reported single-node, eight-GPU
Qwen3-30B-A3B run, full-vocabulary multi-teacher distillation increased peak device memory by
only 17 GB relative to standard DAPO training. Figure 12 illustrates this reconstruction architecture.

S YSTEMIC T RAIN –I NFERENCE C ONSISTENCY

On-policy RL fundamentally dictates that the inference and training engines yield nearly identical
probability distributions for a given policy state. Any systemic deviation skews the importancesampling ratio, silently degrading the optimization process into an unstable off-policy regime.
LoongSage treats this consistency not merely as an algorithmic assumption, but as a continuously observable and localizable systemic property. Potential sources of divergence span multiple
layers: textual re-tokenization mismatches, arithmetic precision truncation, non-equivalent fused
kernels (Dao et al., 2022), mismatched quantization schemes between engines, non-deterministic
discrete decisions (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts routing), and minor variances in parallel reduction or
padding. To counteract this drift, the framework implements a defense-in-depth strategy categorized
into source alignment, precision anchoring, and residual correction (summarized in Figure 13).

Routing Replay (R3) (Ma et al., 2025). Rather than allowing microscopic logit perturbations to

for critical numerical stability.
Algorithmic Residual Correction and Monitoring. Because source alignment cannot fully eliminate hardware-level kernel discrepancies or the weight-version staleness inherent to asynchronous
execution, the algorithm plane provisions a spectrum of residual defenses spanning both token and
sequence granularities. At the fine-grained token level, LoongSage natively supports Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS) (Ionides, 2008; Yao et al., 2025) and Masked Importance Sampling (MIS).
These methods reweight the step-wise loss using the ratio πtrain /πrollout —the discrepancy between
the training engine’s recomputed probabilities and the inference engine’s sampling probabilities—
while clipping or bounding out-of-range values.
To mitigate compounded drift over long-horizon tasks, the framework elevates these defenses via explicit sequence-level corrections, which recalibrate the objective based on the cumulative trajectory
ratio. Furthermore, it integrates advanced sequence-level filtering mechanisms like Second-Moment
Trust Policy Optimization (M2PO) and Off-Policy Sequence Masking (OPSM), which discard or
mask extreme trajectory outliers to prevent pathological samples from dominating a gradient update.
An alternative execution mode treats πrollout as the definitive behavior policy, implicitly absorbing
the gap and bypassing reference log-probability recomputation—an optimization that is structurally
mutually exclusive with TIS, MIS, and M2PO.
Regardless of the chosen configuration, the framework continuously monitors the K3 KL divergence
estimate, the token-clipping ratio, and weight-version staleness spans. While algorithmic clipping
and OPSM act as safeguards against off-policy drift, LoongSage treats aligning critical kernels,
replaying discrete MoE decisions, and pinning precision on critical paths as the primary systemic
remedies.

Beyond aggregate latency reductions, this ablation reveals two critical systemic dynamics. First, the
windowed-fifo strategy achieves a 4%–6% higher throughput than no-window despite maintaining a buffer less than half as deep. It achieves this by enforcing a strict pacing between producer
and consumer, preventing the buffer from being flooded by fast, short-horizon trajectories. Consequently, this ordering constraint incurs zero throughput penalty for this workload while actively
suppressing the system’s length-biased sampling pathology. Hence, it is adopted as the default for
the production runs reported above. Second, relaxing the staleness bound S from 1.0 to 2.0 elevates
the steady-state speedup from 1.36× to 1.40×. However, the deeper buffer requires a longer initialization phase. Thus, the 100-step aggregate speedup barely shifts, and the true end-to-end gain
only converges to the steady-state asymptote as the total step count scales. Ultimately, once SGLang
saturates its configured concurrency, tolerating further staleness yields strictly diminishing returns—
empirically validating the Pareto frontier modeled by the allocation objective in Section 4.2.

C ONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduced LoongSage, a lightweight, agent-native reinforcement learning framework engineered specifically for the systemic complexities of LLM post-training. Under its singlecontroller orchestration model, LoongSage unifies arbitrary agent interactions, multi-turn trajectory
materialization, multi-teacher distillation, and distributed policy optimization into a cohesive, modular architecture.
Built upon this foundation, the framework delivers six architectural contributions: (1) zero-intrusion
TITO integration for diverse white- and black-box agent topologies; (2) staleness-bounded, fully
asynchronous pipeline execution; (3) topology-aware TransferMesh weight synchronization; (4)
memory-multiplexed, full-vocabulary multi-teacher distillation; (5) systemic train–inference consistency maintained by design; and (6) a lightweight, zero-fork core exposing a configuration-driven
plugin system. Collectively, these mechanisms address the engineering bottlenecks imposed by
long-horizon multi-turn rollouts, severe heavy-tail latency, and large-scale distributed resource coordination. Extensive empirical evaluations spanning mathematical reasoning, complex software
engineering, black-box coding agents, and multi-teacher distillation validate that LoongSage drives

agentic training while strictly bounding train–inference divergence and minimizing systemic overhead.
Looking ahead, our roadmap focuses on evolving LoongSage into a comprehensive post-training
ecosystem. To further optimize memory utilization at scale, we will introduce cross-node distributed
storage for multi-teacher weights and native low-precision training primitives. Algorithmically, we
intend to broaden the framework’s unified pipeline to encompass Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO), Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), alongside native support
for omni-modal architectures. We hope LoongSage provides the open-source and academic communities with a highly usable, extensible foundation, accelerating future breakthroughs in large-scale
agentic AI systems.

O PERATIONAL N OTES AND L IMITATIONS

Stabilizing the DeepSeek-V4-Flash SWE training recipe surfaced a range of systemic failures that
are invisible at the algorithmic level, yet determine whether a large-scale distributed job can reach
completion. We categorize these observations to aid future infrastructure developers:
Hard Systemic Failures. These faults abruptly halt execution, manifesting through explicit crash
signals or out-of-memory (OOM) triggers. Examples encountered during scale-up include: numerical instability (NaNs) in early Megatron-Core versions when shared-expert overlap was enabled;
SGLang exhausting device VRAM specifically during topology weight updates; a cuDNN DSA
kernel triggering illegal memory corruption; and a CuTe launch cache silently leaking after several
hundred steps. Furthermore, an FP32 output-layer pinning unexpectedly broke Megatron’s checkpoint saver, while the CPU optimizer exhausted host memory and crashed. At the framework orchestration level, repeated JIT operator recompilation under dynamic batching induced steady host RAM
bloating, and a memory-saver hook conflict between SGLang and Megatron-Core deterministically
crashed the inference engine upon CUDA-graph capture.
Silent Structural and Arithmetic Corruptions. More subtly, these anomalies yield no explicit
crash signals, allowing training to proceed while corrupting the RL objective. For instance, a PDL
dependency anomaly within a FlashInfer CuTe-DSL rmsnorm kernel introduced an asynchronous
timing hazard, silently perturbing normalization statistics and inflating the measured train–inference
gap. In another instance, a parameter had identical tensor shapes across both engines but used different memory layouts, causing weight updates to complete without error yet populate the tensor
with corrupted data. Similarly, an incorrectly implemented clamp in the shared-expert activation
quietly widened the distribution gap. Because such defects fail silently, the policy can degrade for
extended periods while conventional metrics appear normal—underscoring the necessity of continuously monitoring the sampling–training gap rather than verifying it only at bring-up.
These real-world challenges highlight a key realization for the agentic RL era: reinforcement learning acts as a strong amplifier for software and hardware coupling. Minute variances in operator
implementations, non-deterministic CUDA rounding, reduction-order variance in collectives, and
dynamic batch-size adjustments are fed back into the policy via the reward signal. Consequently,
hyperparameters alone are insufficient to fully describe a reproducible RL recipe. The configurations shipped with LoongSage encode the resolutions to these systemic hazards, making them
production-validated deployment blueprints rather than mere algorithmic settings.
Current Architectural Boundaries and Roadmap. Several boundaries of the current implementation define our immediate developmental roadmap. First, the protocol adapters (Section 4.1) for
black-box agents utilizing proprietary APIs (deviating from /v1/chat/completions) are actively being finalized. Second, for certain black-box agents, the framework’s parser cannot yet
deterministically classify whether a new branch stems from context compaction or a spawned subtask, necessitating that subtask segments temporarily remain non-trainable placeholders. Third,
cross-node memory pooling for teacher weights—designed to utilize idle host RAM on training and
rollout nodes as a global distributed weight cache—currently exists as a defined interface awaiting
backend implementation. Fourth, the fully asynchronous mode does not yet match the synchronous
path’s generality regarding heterogeneous multi-data-source routing and multi-agent topologies, and
its asynchronous metric aggregation requires finer precision tuning.

Taken together, these boundaries feed directly into the broader roadmap outlined in Section 6, which

corresponding resolutions will continue to be folded into the shipped recipes. By documenting both
the failures and their fixes in the open, we aim to spare the community the recurring cost of rediscovering them and to invite contributions that harden agentic RL infrastructure at scale.
