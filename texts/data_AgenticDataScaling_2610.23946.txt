How Data Scales in Agentic Reinforcement Learning:
Laws and Synthesis Strategies

Bowei He1,2 , Yankai Chen1,2 , Xiaokun Zhang3 , Changjiang Han1 , Ye Yuan2 ,
Chong Li4 , Xue Liu1,2
1
MBZUAI, 2 McGill University, 3 CityUHK, 4 Columbia University

Abstract
Pretraining scaling laws treat training data as a one-dimensional quantity: a token
count. Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) inherits this scalar abstraction, but
modern synthesis pipelines now generate tasks, environments, and trajectories
independently and at very different costs, turning data into a structured design
space. This raises a question pretraining never had to answer: given a synthesis
budget, which axis should be scaled? We address this question end-to-end with
three tightly coupled contributions. First, we decompose agentic data into three
cardinality axes (task, environment, trajectory), plus a reward-density control
variable, and fit per-axis scaling relations on different large language models
(LLMs) across math+code, SWE, web, and UI/tool application domains. Across
all model sizes, model families, domains, and RL algorithms (PPO and GRPO)
we measured, the scaling exponents order as environment > task > trajectory.
When environment diversity is decomposed into world and environment-structure
diversity, its advantage is mainly driven by the former, while the latter is just
comparable to the task diversity. Second, we use the fitted laws as a common
ruler to price synthesis methods, capturing each method’s efficiency as a per-axis
discount factor and identifying critical synthetic-to-real ratios at which collapse
begins; these ratios differ by an order of magnitude across axes. Third, we cast
budget allocation as a constrained optimization under our laws and discounts,
deriving a Chinchilla-style compute-optimal recipe and validating it at held-out
scale. The empirical results demonstrate that following the recipe yields up to 1.7×
compute efficiency over the dominant “scale-the-trajectories” practice.

Introduction

Existing compute-optimal scaling laws for language model training typically reduce the training data
distribution to a single scalar, the number of tokens, as exemplified by the Chinchilla scaling law [16]
and its predecessor framework [19]. Recent agentic reinforcement learning (RL) works [13, 20, 30]
inherited this single-axis abstraction, plotting performance against rollout tokens, prompt count,
or training steps as if they were interchangeable. But agentic data is fundamentally different. A
single “data point” simultaneously encodes a task specification, an environment with its API surface
and dynamics, a sampled trajectory of model actions and tool feedback, and a reward signal, each
generated by a different pipeline at a different cost. Synthesis pipelines amplify this heterogeneity:
environment generators [4, 14, 50], task generators [36, 59], and trajectory generators [2, 10] act on
different axes and saturate at different rates. The scalar abstraction has quietly become wrong.
This shift even changes what the central question is. With modern synthesis tooling, agentic data is
effectively unbounded in supply, so the practitioner’s problem is no longer “how much more data
should I collect,” but “where should I spend my synthesis budget?” Figure 1 makes this concrete:
two training runs on Qwen3-8B with identical total tokens and FLOPs, differing only in how the

A principled answer to “where to synthesize” requires three conceptual moves, each enabling the next:
(i) decompose the data axis into independent design dimensions and measure each independently;
(ii) price synthesis methods by how efficiently they convert compute into “real-equivalent” data on
each dimension; (iii) solve budget allocation as a constrained optimization under the fitted laws and
discounts. The order is not optional: without per-axis exponents there is no common unit in which to
price synthesis, and without prices there is no objective for the optimization to minimize.
Concretely, we argue that in agentic RL, synthesis turns the data axis from a scalar into a design
space, and the right scaling laws turn that design space into a solvable optimization problem. We
instantiate this argument with three contributions, each enabling the next:
• Decomposing the design space (Section 4). We separate agentic data into three cardinality axes,
task, environment, and trajectory, plus a reward-density control variable, and fit independent scaling
exponents on each axis. Across Qwen3-4B/8B/14B, GRPO and PPO, five domains, and two further
model families (twenty one settings in total), we observe a robust ordering: environment αE >
task αT > trajectory ατ ; resolving the environment axis into world and environment-structure
diversity sharpens it to world αW > environment structure αV ≈ task αT > trajectory ατ .
• Pricing synthesis along each axis (Section 5). Using the fitted laws as a common ruler, we measure
how efficiently existing synthesis methods convert compute into real-equivalent data, capturing
this as a per-axis discount κ ∈ [0, 1] and identifying critical synthetic-to-real ratios r⋆ at which

Related Work

Data scaling laws. In LLM pretraining, Kaplan et al. [19] and Hoffmann et al. [16] established powerlaw relationships between loss, parameters, and tokens, giving rise to compute-optimal training;
2

subsequent work has refined these laws under inference constraints [38], robustness checks [34],
and parameter-counting interpretations [39]. In RL for LLMs, DeepSeek-R1 [13] demonstrated that
pure RL with verifiable rewards scales reasoning capability, ScaleRL [20] characterized RL compute
scaling under a fixed recipe, Mai et al. [30] reported a ZeroTIR-specific “Agent RL Scaling Law”,
and Tan et al. [47] provided an empirical study of RL post-training scaling. In both settings, data
enters as a single axis: tokens in pretraining, and rollout tokens or unique prompts in RL. Rather than
adding a new global axis, we decompose the data axis itself into task, environment, and trajectory
axes, and find that this decomposition is necessary to predict allocation outcomes.
Agentic data synthesis. Recent work synthesizes individual axes of agentic data: environments [4, 14,
50, 56], tasks [36, 48, 59], and trajectories [2, 10, 22, 57]. A growing body of work has documented
synthesis-induced quality degradation, or model collapse, in pretraining [7, 9, 41, 46, 54]. Existing
methods scale a chosen axis in isolation; we provide a framework that prices them on a common
scale, finding their collapse ratios differ by an order of magnitude.
Curriculum and unsupervised environment design. GenEnv [14], self-evolving curricula [3], and
difficulty-aligned task generation [36, 56] address a question our framework deliberately leaves open:
in what order should data along a given axis be presented? Our decomposition operates one level
up, asking how much budget each axis deserves; curriculum operates within an axis, deciding which
slice to sample at each training step. The two are complementary: our recipe specifies the per-axis
budget, and curriculum methods can then schedule consumption of that budget.

Preliminaries and Setup

3.1

Agentic RL and the data tensor

A policy πθ acts in an environment instance e ∈ SE on a task t ∈ ST , producing a trajectory
τ = (o0 , a0 , o1 , a1 , . . . , oH ), where ai are tool or action calls and oi are observations, including
environment feedback. A reward function ρassigns R(τ, t, e) = ρ(τ, t, e). We define the training
corpus as D = ST , SE , {(ti , ei , τi )}ni=1 , ρ , where ST is the finite set of training tasks, SE is
the finite set of training environment instances, and each trajectory τi is associated with a taskenvironment pair (ti , ei ). For later use, let nt,e = { i : ti = t, ei = e } , Ω = {(t, e) : nt,e > 0},
where nt,e is the number of trajectories collected for cell (t, e) and Ω is the set of covered taskenvironment cells.
The data tensor induces three cardinality axes and one control variable. (1) Task axis DT : the
number of unique training task specifications, DT = |ST |. Task specifications are deduplicated by
sentence-embedding cosine distance > 0.15; five alternative rules, including embedding-free ones,
keep αT within [0.238, 0.261] (Appendix G). (2) Environment axis DE : the number of distinct
training environment instances, DE = |SE |. An environment-structure instance is identified by its
structural signature, a canonical hash over the typed tool/API surface, the state schema, the transition
semantics, and the verifier interface; content never changes it, so a procedural seed creates a new
instance only if it changes the signature. The signature is a deduplication criterion, not a claim
that two instances with distinct signatures constitute distinct worlds; variation in the underlying
world, meaning a distinct repository, corpus, app, or site, is reported separately as world diversity
DW (Table 1; Appendix B). (3) Trajectory

Decomposing the Design Space: Per-Axis Scaling Laws

Before deciding where to synthesize data, we first quantify the marginal scaling behavior of each
axis. This section implements the first of the three steps outlined in Section 1: we decompose agentic
data into its three cardinality axes and fit an independent scaling exponent for each.
Functional form. To make the three cardinality axes comparable, we need a functional form in which
each axis contributes its own scaling term and its own exponent. Following the additive power-law
tradition of pretraining scaling laws [16, 19], we posit:

Independent-axis exponents

Protocol. For each axis, we hold the other two at values fixed in advance by a calibration sweep
(doubling an axis changes success by less than 2 points), (DE , DT , Dτ ) = (256, 10,240, 8), reused

Interpretation. Environment diversity is the highest-leverage axis because each new environment
forces the policy to generalize across different state spaces, action vocabularies, and tool-feedback
dynamics, which are structural variation forms that cannot be obtained from new tasks within a
fixed environment; Section 4.4 locates most of this leverage in world diversity. Task diversity is
intermediate: more tasks expose new goal structures, but the underlying interaction grammar is fixed.
Trajectory resampling has the lowest exponent because additional rollouts on already-covered (task,
env) pairs primarily reduce variance within an already-explored region of policy space; once a sample
is informative enough to estimate advantage well, further rollouts give diminishing returns [43, 55].

4.4

Resolving the environment axis: world versus structure

Outside math+code, our pools grow the number of distinct worlds W (repositories, apps, sites) and of
structural variants per world V together, DE = W × V ; the 128 SWE instances, for example, span
11 repositories. Math+code has a single world (a Python tool server), so its αE is a pure structure
exponent († in Table 2). Holding DE , compute, and training signal fixed and moving budget from V
to W , from (W, V ) = (1, 64) to (8, 8) ((4, 16) on web), raises held-out-world success at every step in
SWE, UI/tool, and web, with non-overlapping CIs (e.g., from 18.0 to 26.1% on SWE; Appendix C).
A joint fit gives αW = 0.44 ± 0.04 and αV = 0.28 ± 0.03 (a repository-only SWE sweep gives
αW = 0.46 ± 0.05), so wherever the two separate,
αW (0.44) > αV (0.28) ≈ αT (0.24 to 0.27) > ατ (0.11).
World diversity is the steepest axis we measured, while structure and task diversity are not separable at
our resolution: environment diversity beats task diversity through its world component. The blended
αE is the composition-weighted mixture of αW and αV (on SWE, 0.87 × 0.44 + 0.13 × 0.28 ≈ 0.42
against the measured 0.39±0.03), and without a world axis, structure carries the whole generalization
burden (αV = 0.41 on math+code). Content-only variation is not environment diversity (seeds at a
fixed signature give 0.07 ± 0.02), and structural diversity keeps paying beyond factor-level coverage
up to an inventory ceiling of 384 signatures (Appendix C).

Pricing Synthesis: A Per-Axis Discount Factor

The per-axis exponents fitted in Section 4 provide us with a ruler for how data scales. With that ruler
in hand, we can finally compare heterogeneous synthesis methods on a common scale, asking how
efficiently each one converts compute into real-equivalent data along the axis it targets.
Table 3 maps a representative cross-section of agentic synthesis methods to the axes they primarily
scale. This taxonomy alone organizes a fragmented field by giving every method a shared coordinate
system; the κ and r⋆ columns (defined in Sections 5.1 and 5.2) make the comparison quantitative.
Reduced to a common generate, filter, and train core, axis membership still explains 97% of the
variance of κ (ANOVA F (2, 5) = 76.6, p < 0.001; Appendix I).

Solving Allocation: A Compute-Optimal Recipe

With per-axis laws (Section 4) and per-axis synthesis discounts (Section 5), the question of “what
should I synthesize” is no longer a heuristic call: it becomes a constrained optimization with a
tractable solution. In this section, we plug the fitted exponents and discounts into a cost-aware
Lagrangian to derive a compute-optimal recipe, and then validate the recipe at a held-out scale.

6.3

Held-out validation

To test whether the recipe extrapolates beyond the regime it was fitted on, we predict the optimal
allocation at a compute scale and model size held out from the fit, and verify it with a training run.
Protocol. We fit Eq. 8 using only the four smaller compute levels C ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} × C0 on
Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, then predict the optimal allocation at Ctest = 32C0 on Qwen3-14B, a
held-out point in both compute scale and model size. The prediction yields (DE , DT , Dτ , N ) =
(500, 9,800, 30, 14B) at Ctest . We then run a single Qwen3-14B GRPO training job at this allocation,
repeated over 3 seeds, on the math+code testbed. The predicted DE = 500 exceeds the 384 signatures
our generator inventory can realize (Section 4.4), so the validation pool adds 116 world-structure
pairs in which an existing signature is instantiated on a different world; only 384 of its 500 units are
structurally unique.
Results and analysis. The predicted held-out success rate from Eq. 1 is 51.7% ± 1.6%; the measured
value is 51.3% ± 1.4% over 3 seeds, well within the prediction interval (Fig. 5b, black star). Running
the same training budget under the naive recipe yields 42.9%±1.3%, and under Chinchilla-transferred
47.3% ± 1.5%. Our recipe wins by 4.0 to 8.4 absolute points, corresponding to a 1.4× to 1.7×
compute-efficiency gain: the Chinchilla-transferred allocation would need ≈ 1.4Ctest of compute to
reach our measured success rate, while our recipe matches the naive allocation’s success rate with
only ≈ 0.6Ctest . The recipe is not over-fit to our testbed or cost model: its exponents shift by less
than ±0.04 when re-derived on SWE and web data (Appendix M), by less than 0.05 under ±50%
cost perturbations (Appendix N), and by at most 0.06 (0.7 points of predicted success at C = 32C0 )
when the measured horizon, latency, failure, and utilization costs are added, always toward tasks and
trajectories (Appendix L). Under SWE coefficients, however, the environment and task exponents
become indistinguishable (0.28 vs. 0.29), so we do not claim that DE dominates everywhere; it loses
its top rank only above roughly 3.4× our highest measured fully loaded cost per environment (11.8
USD-equivalent on SWE; 2.9 to 11.8 across four domains and two clusters).
6.4

Two allocation regimes: synthesis versus world acquisition

Eq. 8 allocates DE as our pools grow it; pricing worlds and structures separately (Section 4.4) gives
two regimes. Within a synthesis budget, the choice is between structural variants and tasks: where
a world axis separates, their exponents are not separable (αV = 0.28 vs. αT = 0.24 to 0.27) but
unit costs differ 23-fold (4.10 vs. 0.18 USD), so task synthesis is the better purchase per dollar
and structural variation pays mainly until the world pool’s structural space is covered, after which
the inventory ceiling binds (in math+code, where αV = 0.41 > αT , Eq. 8 already prices this).
For procuring new worlds, αW is the steepest exponent, and acquisition ranks first per dollar until
the fully loaded cost per world exceeds about 3.4× our highest per-environment cost, roughly 40
USD-equivalent. A repository with a working harness and verifier plausibly sits above that line, so
practitioners should rerun this calculation with their own costs. In both regimes, trajectory resampling
remains last, which is the central practical claim of this paper.

7

Conclusions and Future Work

We have argued and shown that agentic RL data is not a scalar but a structured design space whose
axes carry distinct scaling exponents and distinct synthesis discounts. Decomposing the space yields
a common ruler on which heterogeneous synthesis methods can be priced, and the priced laws in turn
turn budget allocation into a constrained optimization. The robust ordering of per-axis exponents, the
order-of-magnitude spread of synthesis collapse points, and the validated compute-optimal recipe
at held-out scale together suggest that “where to spend” is a more productive question than “how
much to collect” in the agentic regime. Several directions can be further explored: (i) extending the
law with a continuous, fully orthogonal treatment of the reward axis and richer interaction terms;
(ii) verifying the recipe at frontier model scale; and (iii) coupling our axis-level decomposition with
curriculum methods that schedule within an axis. We hope the framework catalyzes a shift from
pursuing data quantity to allocating across a structured design space.

Experimental Setup

We introduce our experimental setup from multiple aspects as follows.
Models. All experiments use Qwen3 base checkpoints [53] at 4B, 8B, and 14B parameters. We
use the base (non-instruct) variants throughout to isolate the effect of RL training data; preliminary
experiments with instruct variants gave qualitatively identical scaling-law exponents (within ±0.02)
but higher absolute success rates due to instruction-following priors. For the model-family check of
Section 4.3, we additionally run reduced 4-point sweeps on Llama-3.1-8B [11] and Qwen2.5-7B [52]
with the hyperparameters below, without retuning (Appendix D).
Hyperparameters. For GRPO we use group size G = 8, learning rate 1×10−6 with linear warmup
over the first 10% of steps and constant thereafter, KL coefficient β = 0.001 on a frozen reference
policy initialized from the base model, clip ratio ϵ = 0.2, and global batch size 256 prompts.
Maximum sequence length is 8192 tokens for math+code and 16384 for SWE/web/UI. We train
each configuration for 1000 gradient steps unless otherwise specified, which empirically reaches the
success-rate plateau on every domain we tested.
PPO. PPO uses the same settings plus a critic. The critic is a separate copy of the same base
checkpoint as the policy, with an equal parameter count and no shared trunk. Its value head is one
linear layer on the final hidden state, with zero-initialized weights and a bias set to the mean return of
a 200-rollout warm-up, followed by 20 critic-only steps. Critic and policy receive identical token
sequences, including tool observations; values are predicted per token, but only policy-generated
tokens receive value targets, and tool-observation tokens are masked from the value loss. We use a
discount factor of 1.0, GAE λ = 0.95, value-loss coefficient 0.5, value clipping at 0.2, critic learning
rate 1×10−6 , and one critic epoch per policy epoch. Rewards are not whitened: the reward is binary (0
or 1) with a −0.1 format penalty, advantages are batch-normalized after GAE, and the KL coefficient
to the frozen base-model reference is β = 0.001. At convergence, the critic’s explained variance is
0.61 ± 0.04 and its TD residual is stationary over the last 200 steps.
Infrastructure. Training uses the verl framework [44] with VerlTool [17] for multi-tool rollout.
Rollouts are run on vLLM 0.6.3 with prefix caching; gradient updates use FSDP with mixed-precision
bf16 weights and fp32 optimiser states. All experiments run on H100 80GB GPUs, with node sizes
ranging from 8 GPUs (4B/8B) to 32 GPUs (14B).
Datasets per domain. Math+code (TIR): training prompts are 12k synthetic math problems following the ZeroTIR recipe [23, 30]; evaluation on AIME24/25 [28] (30 problems each), MATH500 [15],
and AMC23 [29] (40 problems). SWE: training on SWE-Gym [33] (2.4k issues across 11 repos);
evaluation on the 500 instances of SWE-bench Verified [18]. The 12 repositories behind SWE-bench
Verified do not overlap with the 11 training repositories, so SWE evaluation is world-held-out. Web:
training on WebArena-Lite [58] sub-environments (5 sites × 200 tasks); evaluation on a held-out
partition of 240 tasks. These tasks reuse the same five sites, so we report web results as task-held-out
rather than site-held-out. UI/tool: training on AppWorld [48] “train” split (250 tasks × 9 apps) and
ALFWorld [45]; evaluation on the AppWorld test partition, whose challenge split contains 417 tasks
that each require at least one app designated unseen at training time. Search: a retrieval tool over
a Wikipedia dump plus a paginated web-search API; evaluation on Bamboogle [35] and a held-out
multi-hop split. Environment instances differ in the retrieval backend (BM25 [37], a dense retriever,
or the paginated web-search API), in snippet grammar and rate-limit semantics, and in whether a
page-fetch tool exists. This domain is used for the robustness check of Section 4.3 on Qwen3-8B. For
axes that require synthesis we use AWM [50] for environments, AutoPlay [36] / SCA [59] for tasks,
and standard policy-rollout for trajectories.

Environment Construction and Statistics

Structural signature. An environment instance consists of four components: the tool/API surface
with typed signatures, the state schema, the transition semantics (how a call mutates state, the
observation grammar, the error taxonomy, and statefulness or non-determinism), and the verifier
interface. Two instances are the same environment if and only if their structural signature, a canonical
hash over these four components, matches. Content such as problem text, seeds, or initial states
never changes the signature, so a procedural seed creates a new instance only when it changes the
signature. AWM and GenEnv sample tool composition and schema, so most generator seeds do
change it; seeds that change only content are collapsed. The signature is a deduplication criterion and
does not claim that instances with distinct signatures are distinct worlds; Appendix C treats world
diversity separately.
Per-domain instantiation. In math+code (TIR), an environment is the Python tool server. Instances differ, for example, in a stateless python_exec versus a persistent REPL in which variables
survive across turns, a one-tool server versus a three-tool server (python_exec, sympy_solve,
unit_check) that requires piping between tools, a whitelist that raises ImportError on numpy, or
a 200-character stdout cap that requires pagination. A new math problem is a task. In SWE, an
environment is a configuration of container image, dependency graph, build and test harness, and
agent toolset. Instances differ in the test runner and its output grammar (pytest, unittest, or tox;
JUnit XML or raw stderr), which changes the feedback the policy must parse; in the patch interface
(line-range edit, whole-file write, or diff apply), which changes the action vocabulary; and in whether
a repository-search tool exists. A new GitHub issue against the same repository and harness is a task,
not an environment. Web and UI/tool environments are sites and MCP-style servers with distinct
action spaces, entity schemas and business logic, authentication and permission models, pagination
semantics, and error taxonomies. The search domain varies the retrieval backend and its result
grammar (Appendix A).
Pool statistics. Table 6 characterizes each domain’s pool at its largest grid point. Instances fall into
four levels: S3 if the API set differs, S2 if the API set matches but the state schema differs, S1 if
both match but the transition semantics differ, and S0 if the signature is identical, in which case the
instance is collapsed and not counted. Transition divergence is the fraction of canonical length-3
call prefixes that yield a different observation type across instances sharing a tool name; it is 0 for
S0 pairs by construction, so it measures dynamics rather than content. No counted instance differs
only in seed or content. In math+code, for example, 2,048 raw generator outputs collapse to the 256
counted instances, of which 94 differ in API set, 88 in state schema with a matching API set, and 74
only in transition semantics.

Task-environment binding. Tasks and environments are not orthogonal, and Eq. 1 does not assume

fraction of the task × environment grid is 78%, 54%, 41%, and 63%, respectively. The residual

environment diversity gates task diversity, is a result rather than a nuisance.

C

World and Structure Diversity

Three levels and pool composition. Table 1 distinguishes content (L0), environment structure (L1),
and world (L2); content variation is never counted as environment diversity. Our pools contain
different amounts of world variation. Math+code has a single world, a Python tool server, so all of
its instances are L1 variants and its αE should be read as an environment-structure exponent. The
128 SWE instances are built over 11 real repositories with roughly 12 structural variants each, so
DE = 128 counts structures rather than worlds; what the definition does fix is the boundary that a
new issue against an unchanged repository and harness is a task. Web instances span the 5 WebArena
sites. Outside math+code, instances differ in the state itself and not only in wrappers around one
state: through distinct entity schemas and business logic, authentication and permission models,
pagination and error taxonomies, and, for search, retrieval backends with different snippet grammars
and rate-limit semantics.

the number of distinct worlds W and the number of structural variants per world V , evaluating on
held-out worlds (SWE-bench Verified for SWE and the unseen-app split for UI/tool). SWE uses
repositories as worlds with (W, V ) ∈ {(1, 64), (2, 32), (4, 16), (8, 8)}, where W runs to 8 rather than
11 to keep the grid dyadic; UI/tool uses apps (8 of the 9 in AppWorld) on the same grid; and web uses
its 5 sites with the reduced grid {(1, 64), (2, 32), (4, 16)}. To reach V = 64 within a single world,
we expanded the per-world variant pool with AWM beyond the roughly 12 variants per repository of
the Table 2 pool, with signature deduplication on the same criterion. W is capped by the number
of real worlds we can obtain rather than by budget, and we do not extrapolate past it. Trajectories,

Results. We give the fit-free reading first. At every grid transition in all three domains, moving budget
from V to W at fixed DE improves held-out-world success, with no overlapping intervals within any

and αV = 0.28 ± 0.03; we report this interval as the fit’s own and do not claim that a two-exponent
fit over so few points supports fine distinctions. A repository-only sweep on SWE at fixed harness,
W ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 11}, independently gives αW = 0.46 ± 0.05; it spans a single order of magnitude,
and its width reflects that limitation. The substitution rate at the (4, 16) operating point, defined as
the factor by which V must be multiplied to match the held-out-world gain from doubling W , is
approximately 3.0; it is a local rate rather than a global constant.
Mixture check. On a pool that grows W and V together, which is what AWM and GenEnv do when
run as intended, the effective slope in log DE is the composition-weighted mixture of αW and αV . In
SWE, the sweep of Table 2 spans W by about 11× but V by only about 1.5×, so roughly 0.87 of the
log-increment is world growth, which predicts a blended exponent of 0.87×0.44+0.13×0.28 ≈ 0.42
against the measured 0.39 ± 0.03. In math+code the world weight is zero, so the blend reduces to αV
itself, which is why that domain reads 0.41. The structural return is therefore domain-dependent, from
0.28 pooled over SWE, UI/tool, and web to 0.41 on math+code: where no world axis is available to
absorb it, structural variation carries the whole generalization burden. For a practitioner who scales
such a pool, the blended αE remains the right number, but it is never the return on world diversity
alone.
Transfer to unseen worlds. Because SWE-bench Verified and AppWorld’s challenge split are
world-held-out (Appendix A), the structural gains we report on SWE and AppWorld already measure
generalization to worlds that were never acquired for training. Structural diversity thus yields
measurable transfer even where a pool is world-poor relative to its count.
Factor inventory and coverage. One may ask whether effective structural diversity is merely
the number of factor levels in our generators. After canonicalizing each domain’s generator into
four domain-specific structural-factor slots, the observed level vector is (4, 4, 3, 8) in each of SWE,
UI/tool, and web: execution or interaction mode (4 levels), tool/API or action-surface family (4),
observation or verifier-output grammar (3), and state, dependency, or error-semantics regime (8). The
realizable inventory is therefore 4 × 4 × 3 × 8 = 384 signature-distinct structural combinations per
domain, and the median normalized factor-space Hamming distance among sampled instances is
0.75. A level-isolating construction first covers every observed level at DE = 16 (pool A), and a
pair-complete construction first covers every observed level pair at DE = 48 (pool B); both coverage
points lie well below the sampled pool C (|C| = 256) and the top sweep value DE = 384. Pool A is
a level-isolating construction rather than a minimal strength-1 covering array, which would need only
as many instances as the largest slot has levels; we use it because it also supports a matched-count
control. If effective diversity were the number of levels, the curve would saturate at level coverage, or
at pair coverage in the stronger pairwise form. Neither occurs. The local log-log slopes over DE = 4
to 384 are 0.44, 0.42, 0.41, 0.40, 0.38, 0.37, and 0.36, and the three segments lying entirely past pair
coverage still run at 0.38, 0.37, and 0.36. Regressing held-out success on the logarithm of levels
covered and log DE jointly, the partial contribution of log DE given level coverage is ∆R2 = 0.18
(p = 0.004). Directly, held-out success is 27.8, 31.6, and 34.5% for pools A, B, and C: A trails C
by 6.7 points despite being level-complete, and B trails C by 2.9 points despite being pair-complete.

D

Sweep Protocol and Fitting Methodology

Per-axis sweep protocol. For each axis we collect 6 nested grid points spanning 32×, with 3 random
seeds per point (different seeds for both rollout and gradient noise): DE ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256},
DT ∈ {320, 640, 1280, 2560, 5120, 10240}, and Dτ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} on Qwen3-8B math+code.
The stability extension adds DE ∈ {4, 384}, DT ∈ {160, 20480}, and Dτ ∈ {3, 48} (54 new runs),
so that every sweep has 8 points spanning 48× to 128×. The other two axes are held at the calibrated
values of Appendix A. Each (axis, scale) cell consumes between 0.5 and 20 H100-GPU-hours
depending on the axis sweep; total cost of the per-axis sweeps is reported in Appendix Q.
Nesting and subset sensitivity. Pools are nested across adjacent grid points, E8 ⊂ E16 ⊂ · · · ⊂ E256
and T320 ⊂ · · · ⊂ T10240 , and the Dτ sweep reuses the same cell pool. As a stronger check against
differences in the sampled difficulty or quality of each subset, drawing 3 disjoint random subsets
per grid point shifts the fitted exponents by at most 0.01 (αE from 0.41 to 0.40; αT from 0.26 to
0.27), with a per-point success spread of at most 0.8 points. Base-policy pass@8 is also flat along the
sweeps (0.186 to 0.199 across DE and 0.181 to 0.203 across DT ).
D.1

Training-signal accounting

A cell draw is one task-environment pair rolled out G times. Every grid point of the DE and DT
sweeps uses |Ω| = 256,000 cells, each drawn once, with 256 draws per step, 1,000 updates, and
Dτ = G = 8. Increasing DE therefore redistributes the same cells across more environments
(Table 9): trajectories, distinct task specifications, and updates are constant, rollout tokens drift by
less than 4%, and normalized FLOPs stay between 0.98 and 1.02. Along DT , rollout tokens drift by
2.1% at the same constant trajectory and update counts, while the Dτ sweep scales rollout tokens
32× at a constant number of updates. Per-environment repetition falls 32× along DE (32,000 visits
per environment at DE = 8 against 1,000 at DE = 256), so the steepest exponent is obtained with
the least per-environment signal.

Fitting methodology

Functional form and estimator. We fit
L(D) = K D−α + L∞ ,

(9)

with parameters (K, α, L∞ ), by nonlinear weighted least squares. Because L∞ enters nonlinearly, we
solve the problem by profiling L∞ out (Algorithm 1): for each candidate L∞ on a 400-point grid over
[−0.5, 1.0], the inner problem log(L−L∞ ) = log K −α log D is linear in (log K, α) and is solved in
closed form by weighted least squares on log-loss residuals, with weights wi = 1/σ̂i2 , where σ̂i is the
sample standard error at grid point i over 3 seeds; we keep the candidate with the smallest weighted
residual. Section 4.1 and this paragraph describe the same, single estimator. As an independent check,
a Levenberg-Marquardt fit [21, 32] of Eq. 9 in raw space gives (αE , αT , ατ ) = (0.415, 0.264, 0.107),
within 0.006 of the reported values. On synthetic data with known ground truth, the estimator recovers
α to within ±0.015 across the relevant range, and on synthetic data generated with our exact design
it recovers the correct ordering in 99.8% of replicas.
Confidence intervals. 95% CIs are computed by paired bootstrap: for each of 1000 resamples we
sample 6 grid points with replacement and 3 seeds with replacement, refit, and record (K, α, L∞ ).
The CI for α is the range from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the bootstrap distribution. We
chose the paired bootstrap over the delta method because the latter underestimates uncertainty in
small-sample regimes (n = 18 per fit).
Goodness-of-fit diagnostics. For all per-axis fits reported in Table 2, the weighted R2 exceeds
0.97. Residuals show no systematic pattern with D (Durbin-Watson statistic in [1.7, 2.3] across
all 18 fits, consistent with no autocorrelation). We also tested an alternative two-term form L =
K1 D−α1 + K2 D−α2 + L∞ ; the BIC favours the single-term form (Eq. 9) at every scale we measured,
indicating the data does not support a two-regime fit.

Model-Size Exponent Fit

The main paper’s law (Eq. 1) is fitted with (DE , DT , Dτ ) at fixed N = 8B. To incorporate model
size into Section 6’s allocation problem, we additionally fit the marginal N -axis exponent.
Protocol. Holding (DE , DT , Dτ ) at saturation values for each model size, we sweep N ∈
{0.6, 1.7, 4, 8, 14, 32} B (using Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B/4B/8B/14B and Qwen3-32B [53] as the largest
point) and fit L = KN N −αN + L∞ following the same protocol as Appendix D.
Result. The fitted exponent is αN = 0.30 ± 0.04 (95% CI), with KN = 0.85 and L∞ jointly fitted
to the same value as the data-axis fits (L∞ = 0.10 ± 0.03). This αN is materially smaller than
Chinchilla’s data-axis exponent (∼ 0.5), consistent with the observation in Section 6.2 that agentic
RL benefits more from data variety than from raw model scale in the regime we measured.
Range of validity. We caution that the N -sweep covers only 0.6B to 32B; extrapolation to frontier
scales (≥ 70B) is unverified and is listed as a limitation in Section 7 and Appendix R.

Task Individuation: Threshold, Definition, and Human Validation

Threshold. Task counts DT are deduplicated by sentence-embedding cosine distance > θ (Section 3.1); we ablate θ ∈ {0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30}. Embeddings are computed with the gteQwen2-1.5B encoder [24]. Across this range, the fitted αT varies between 0.245 and 0.272 on
the math+code testbed at 8B (Table 14), with the chosen default (θ = 0.15) at 0.261 ± 0.02. The
qualitative ordering αE > αT > ατ is preserved across all five thresholds. At low θ (e.g., 0.05),
near-duplicates inflate DT but contribute little gradient information, slightly biasing the fitted α
downward. At high θ (e.g., 0.30), the deduplication is so aggressive that we remove genuinely distinct
tasks, slightly biasing it upward. The plateau at θ ∈ [0.10, 0.20] is robust, justifying our default
choice.
Definition. The threshold ablation tests numerical sensitivity but not the definition itself, which is
the sharper question: semantically different instructions can require nearly identical skills, while
superficially similar ones can require different reasoning or tool-use patterns. We therefore refit αT
under five alternative individuation rules, two of them embedding-free (Table 15): the raw count
without deduplication; the default rule with a different encoder (e5-mistral-7b [49]); a skill signature
that deduplicates by the tool-call skeleton (tool types plus argument types) of a reference solution; a
verifier signature that deduplicates by the structure of the verification program; and a human-validated
pool. All six values lie in [0.238, 0.261], strictly between ατ and αE under every rule.
Human validation. We sampled 500 task pairs stratified by embedding distance, and three annotators,
volunteers from MBZUAI who participated without compensation, labeled each pair for whether
it requires a materially different skill or tool-use pattern (Fleiss’ kappa 0.68 [8]). Agreement with
the majority label is 87.2% for the skill signature (Cohen’s kappa 0.74 [5]), 84.6% for the verifier
signature (kappa 0.69), 80.4% for the embedding rule (kappa 0.61, with 11.2% false merges), and
68.0% for the raw count (kappa 0.36). The embedding rule is substantial but imperfect and the skill
signature is better, so we use the skill-signature refit as the primary robustness check and keep the
embedding rule as the cheap operational default. The ordering does not depend on which rule is used.

Reward Density as a Control Variable

The main paper treats DR as a control variable with two categories (sparse outcome reward and
dense process reward) and does not claim a reward scaling law comparable to those of the three
cardinality axes. Here we report its marginal sensitivity under a continuous treatment via the number
of process-reward signals per trajectory.
Protocol. We instantiate process rewards using a Qwen3-8B-based PRM trained following [25] on a
held-out math+code corpus. For each trajectory, we extract k ∈ {0, 1, 3, 8} process-reward signals
(placed at uniformly spaced positions across the trajectory length) and append them as additional
return-to-go terms. k = 0 recovers sparse outcome reward; k = 8 provides dense step-level
supervision.
Marginal sensitivity to reward density. Fitting L = KR (k + 1)−αR + L∞ on the math+code
testbed at 8B yields
αR = 0.18 ± 0.03, KR = 0.41, L∞ = 0.11.
(11)
We report αR as the marginal sensitivity of the loss to reward density on this testbed, not as a
scaling exponent on par with those of the cardinality axes; numerically, it falls between the trajectory
(ατ ≈ 0.11) and task (αT ≈ 0.26) exponents.
Implications. Reward density and trajectory count interact. Holding the total reward-signal budget
k · Dτ fixed, we observe approximate substitutability: doubling k at half Dτ produces equivalent
loss within ±0.5 pp on the same compute. This justifies treating DR as an effective multiplier on Dτ
in Section 6.2’s allocation, rather than as an independent axis. We leave a fully orthogonal treatment
of reward density to future work.

Cost-Coefficient Sensitivity

To verify the recipe is not over-fit to our specific cost calibration (Appendix L), we perturb each
coefficient ±50% and re-solve the Lagrangian.
The largest sensitivity is to cE (the environment-synthesis cost), which makes sense intuitively: cE
sets the price of the highest-leverage axis, and small changes propagate. Even so, all perturbations
stay within ±0.05 of the default exponents, and the qualitative ordering is preserved in every row of
Table 21. Because each κi enters the allocation problem only through ci /κi , these perturbations also
cover the corresponding misestimates of the synthesis discounts. We conclude the recipe is robust to
reasonable mis-specification of cost coefficients; Appendix L complements these perturbations with
structurally richer cost models.

Limitation Analysis

This work has several limitations. First, although we evaluate the proposed axis-decomposed scaling
framework across multiple model sizes, algorithms, domains, and model families, the experiments
remain limited to the specific model families, benchmark environments, and compute scales studied in
this paper. In particular, the model-size sweep covers open-weight models in the sub-frontier regime,
and the held-out validation is performed at a single larger scale. Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B
30

preserve the ordering of the exponents while shifting their absolute values by up to 0.05, and the
fitted exponents and compute-optimal allocation recipe may change at frontier scale, under different
pretraining distributions, or for agents with substantially different tool-use and reasoning priors.
Therefore, the ordering is our claim, and the numerical exponents reported in this paper should be
viewed as empirical estimates for the measured regime rather than universal constants.
Second, our decomposition of agentic data into task, environment, and trajectory axes plus a rewarddensity control variable is a useful abstraction, but it is not perfectly orthogonal. In practice, tasks
and environments often co-vary: a new environment may induce new task types, and a new task
distribution may require different environment structure; our sweeps re-bind a fixed task pool to
compatible environments (Appendix B), but only part of the task × environment grid is realizable.
Similarly, reward density can interact with trajectory diversity because denser rewards may change
which rollouts are informative. We model some cross-axis interactions, especially between task and
environment diversity, but richer higher-order interactions may exist. Future work should study more
expressive interaction terms and determine when the additive power-law approximation breaks down.
Third, the resolution of the environment axis into world and structure diversity rests on limited world
counts. World diversity is swept over only 1 to 8 real worlds on SWE and UI/tool and 1 to 4 on
web (1 to 11 repositories in the SWE-only sweep), so αW spans a single order of magnitude. The
structure axis has a finite realizable ceiling under our factor inventory. The structural exponent αV
varies across domains from 0.28 to 0.41, and we do not offer a single value. We do not claim that
structure and world variation are interchangeable, and we do not claim that any of the fitted relations
continue past the measured ranges.
Fourth, the loss-equivalent metric used in this paper is based on held-out task success. This makes
scaling-law fitting practical for verifiable-reward RL, but it does not capture every property relevant
to agent quality. For example, two agents with similar success rates may differ in robustness,
calibration, latency, safety, interpretability, or the severity of their failures. The metric also depends
on the coverage and difficulty distribution of the held-out evaluation set. As a result, improvements
predicted by the fitted law should not be interpreted as comprehensive improvements in all downstream
deployment settings.
Fifth, our treatment of synthetic data quality is necessarily approximate. The synthesis discount
factor κ and collapse ratio r⋆ provide a common way to compare methods, but they compress many
dimensions of quality into a small number of scalar quantities. Synthetic environments, tasks, and
trajectories may differ in realism, diversity, difficulty, distributional coverage, and susceptibility to
model-generated artifacts. These factors may vary across domains and over training time. In addition,
the measured discount for a synthesis method depends on the base model, generator, verifier, and
filtering pipeline used in the experiment; we fix these through a common harness (Appendix I), but
different synthesis pipelines may yield different effective discounts even when targeting the same
axis.
Sixth, the compute-allocation recipe relies on a simplified cost model. We assume an approximately
linear, axis-additive cost structure with per-axis cost coefficients and synthesis discounts. Real
training systems may have non-linear bottlenecks due to rollout latency, tool execution, environment simulation, distributed training overhead, memory constraints, data storage, or human review
costs. Adding the main measured drivers changes recipe exponents by at most 0.06 but makes
the environment and task exponents indistinguishable under SWE coefficients (Appendix L). The
recipe is therefore best understood as a principled first-order allocation rule rather than an exact
prescription for every infrastructure setting. Practitioners should recalibrate the cost coefficients and
fitted exponents under their own hardware, software stack, and data-generation pipeline before using
the recipe operationally.
Seventh, our experiments focus on benchmark-style domains with controlled evaluation and relatively
clear reward signals. Many real-world agentic tasks involve ambiguous objectives, partial observability, long-horizon dependencies, dynamic user preferences, and non-stationary environments. In such
settings, defining task success and reward density is more difficult, and synthetic data may introduce
artifacts that are harder to detect. Extending the framework to open-ended real-world applications
will require stronger evaluation protocols, safety checks, and methods for measuring distribution
shift.

31

Finally, this work does not provide a complete safety or deployment framework for agentic systems.
The proposed scaling laws can make agentic RL training more efficient, but they do not guarantee
that the resulting agents are safe, fair, private, secure, or aligned with user intent. These concerns
require separate evaluations and safeguards. Future work should combine axis-level data scaling with
robustness testing, adversarial evaluation, privacy analysis, fairness auditing, and deployment-time
monitoring.

S

Ethics Statement

This work is an empirical study of data scaling and synthesis allocation in agentic reinforcement
learning. The experiments are conducted on benchmark-style math, code, web, UI/tool, and search
environments, using scripted training pipelines and verifiable or process-based reward signals. We
do not intentionally collect or use private personal data, and the synthetic tasks, environments, and
trajectories studied in this paper are intended for research evaluation rather than for deployment in
real user-facing decision systems. To reduce privacy risk, experimental logs should be stored securely,
inspected for unintended sensitive content where applicable, and released only after appropriate
filtering or anonymization.
The work involves synthetic data generation along multiple axes, including tasks, environments, and
trajectories. Synthetic data can amplify biases, encode unrealistic assumptions, or produce artifacts
that affect downstream model behavior. Our measurements focus on aggregate scaling behavior
and task success, and they should not be interpreted as evidence that a trained agent is safe, fair, or
reliable in deployment. Any use of the proposed allocation recipe in real applications should include
additional evaluations for robustness, bias, privacy leakage, security, and subgroup-level performance.
Because agentic systems can interact with tools, websites, code execution environments, or simulated
applications, there are potential dual-use risks. More efficient agentic RL training could improve
beneficial automation, but it could also lower the cost of training agents for harmful activities such
as phishing, spam, vulnerability discovery, unauthorized data collection, or manipulation of online
services. Our experiments should be run in sandboxed environments with controlled tool access,
and released artifacts should avoid enabling direct misuse. We encourage practitioners to pair these
techniques with access controls, abuse monitoring, rate limits, permission boundaries, and adversarial
safety evaluations.
We also consider fairness and labor-related issues. Apart from a small annotation of task-pair
similarity by three volunteer annotators from MBZUAI who participated without compensation
(Appendix G), which labels properties of synthetic tasks rather than of the annotators themselves,
the paper does not rely on new human-subject experiments or sensitive demographic annotations. If
future extensions use human annotators, user interaction traces, or real-world application data, they
should follow informed-consent procedures, provide appropriate compensation, minimize exposure to
harmful content, and comply with applicable institutional and legal requirements. Similarly, if agentic
systems trained using these methods are deployed in socially consequential domains, humans should
retain meaningful oversight and affected users should have mechanisms for appeal or correction.
Overall, this paper provides a framework for measuring and allocating agentic RL data, not a
guarantee of responsible deployment. The ethical use of this work depends on the context in which
the resulting agents are trained and applied. We recommend that downstream users treat the proposed
scaling laws and synthesis strategies as one component of a broader responsible-development process
that includes privacy protection, security review, fairness auditing, misuse prevention, and transparent
reporting of data sources and evaluation limitations.

T

Broader Impacts

This work studies how data scales in agentic reinforcement learning and proposes a framework
for allocating synthesis budget across tasks, environments, trajectories, and reward density. The
potential positive societal impact is that more efficient data allocation can reduce the compute and
financial cost of training capable agentic systems. By identifying which forms of data provide the
highest marginal return, the proposed framework may help researchers and practitioners train useful
agents with fewer resources, improving accessibility for academic labs and smaller organizations.
More efficient agentic RL could also accelerate progress on beneficial applications such as software
32

engineering assistance, scientific problem solving, education, accessibility tools, and automation of
repetitive digital workflows. In addition, the explicit decomposition of agentic data may improve
transparency: rather than treating training data as an undifferentiated token budget, our framework
encourages practitioners to document what kinds of tasks, environments, trajectories, and reward
signals are used.
At the same time, the same efficiency gains could enable harmful or unintended uses. More capable
and cheaper-to-train agents may be misused for automated disinformation campaigns, generation
of fake online profiles, scalable phishing or social engineering, spam, scraping, surveillance, or
other forms of cyber abuse. Because our results suggest that environment and task synthesis can
be especially high-leverage, a malicious actor could use similar methods to generate many realistic
interaction settings for training agents to operate across websites, applications, or tool interfaces. The
work therefore should not be interpreted as an endorsement of unrestricted deployment of agentic
systems. Practical use of these techniques should be accompanied by safety evaluations, access
controls, monitoring for abuse, and domain-specific restrictions where agent actions can affect real
users or systems.
There are also fairness and representational concerns. Synthetic tasks and environments may encode
the assumptions, biases, or omissions of the systems and datasets used to generate them. If agentic RL
systems trained under such distributions are deployed in settings involving hiring, lending, education,
healthcare, policing, or access to public services, they could make or support decisions that unfairly
affect particular demographic or socioeconomic groups. Our scaling laws measure aggregate task
success and do not by themselves guarantee equitable performance across subpopulations, languages,
regions, or accessibility needs. Any downstream deployment should therefore include disaggregated
evaluation, bias auditing, human oversight, and clear limits on the decisions agents are allowed to
make autonomously.
Privacy is another important consideration. Agentic environments often involve realistic user data,
application states, logs, tool outputs, or simulated personal information. Even when data is synthetic,
it may be derived from real data sources or may resemble sensitive user records. Care is required
to avoid memorization, leakage, or reconstruction of private information through generated tasks,
environments, trajectories, or reward traces. Practitioners applying this framework should use data
minimization, anonymization where appropriate, secure storage of trajectories and logs, and privacy
reviews for any environment that models real users or organizations.
Finally, security risks arise because agentic RL systems interact with tools and external environments.
Training agents to generalize across diverse environments may improve their usefulness, but it
may also improve their ability to discover vulnerabilities, bypass interface constraints, or execute
unintended actions. Synthetic environments should therefore be sandboxed, tool permissions should
be least-privilege, and evaluations should include adversarial and misuse-oriented testing. We view
our contribution as a measurement and allocation framework, not as a complete safety framework.
Future work should integrate the proposed data-scaling perspective with stronger safeguards for
alignment, robustness, privacy, security, and responsible deployment.
