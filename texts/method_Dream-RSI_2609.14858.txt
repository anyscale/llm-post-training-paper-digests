Recursive self-improvement is becoming essential for autonomous AI agents, whose progress depends
on discovering high-value solutions across complex domains. Effective exploration drives this process,
yet managing and improving exploration strategies remains a major bottleneck. Current systems
face a fundamental dilemma: fixed strategies fail to adapt as search spaces scale, while online policy
optimization must navigate vast meta-search spaces under delayed, expensive feedback from longhorizon rollouts. We introduce Dream-RSI, a framework for scalable, recursively self-improving
exploration. A lightweight orchestration layer makes exploration explicit and programmable while
leaving the underlying base agent unchanged. Our key insight is that accumulated discovery history
can act as a replay simulator over the realized search space. By dreaming within this simulator built
from historical discovery trees, Dream-RSI obtains immediate, low-cost off-policy feedback to evaluate
and refine exploration policies without repeated, expensive online evaluation. The improved policy
is then redeployed online to drive further discovery, continuously expanding the simulator pool in a
self-improving loop. Across 9 tasks in 4 domains, Dream-RSI achieves competitive quality and improves
discovery efficiency in several settings.

1. Introduction
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) has emerged as an ambitious goal for autonomous AI systems (Liu
et al., 2026c). A common mechanism underlying RSI is an iterative loop wherein agents propose
candidate solutions, evaluate outcomes, and fold the resulting feedback into subsequent iterations.
Such loops have driven substantial progress across algorithm design (Novikov et al., 2025; RomeraParedes et al., 2024), open-ended mathematical optimization (Anthropic, 2026; Georgiev et al., 2025),
systems design (Cao et al., 2026; Jaber and Jaber, 2026), and agent self-improvement (Lee et al.,
2026; Zhang et al., 2026b,c; Zheng et al., 2026a). As agents grow stronger and self-improvement
targets grow harder, discovery demands long-horizon exploration over vast search spaces, often
spanning thousands of proposal-evaluation cycles (OpenAI, 2026; Ye et al., 2026). At this scale,
orchestrating exploration becomes critical (Zheng et al., 2026b): poor exploration wastes computation
and time, sharply limiting the efficiency and scalability of RSI.
Existing approaches largely rely on manually designed exploration strategies that stay fixed
throughout discovery (Du et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026; Novikov et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026b;
Ye et al., 2026). Such strategies cannot learn from accumulated experience and may repeatedly
spend computation on unproductive search directions. Recent work therefore attempts to optimize
exploration policies online (Liu et al., 2026a), but this faces two fundamental bottlenecks. First,
meta-level feedback is delayed and expensive. Unlike evaluating a single candidate, assessing an
exploration policy requires observing how it shapes discovery over many proposal–evaluation cycles.
Second, the meta-policy space is vast, so many alternatives must be tried before a good one is found.

Together, these make meta-level improvement costly. Each policy demands a long online rollout
before yielding useful signal, making the self-improvement loop hard to close at the exploration layer.
To address these bottlenecks, we want to design a fast and cheap simulator of discovery to prune
exploration policies before costly online deployment. Surprisingly, completed discovery histories
already provide such a simulator. Prior work treats past history merely as static textual context (Hu
et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026b) or as training data for fine-tuning (Wang et al., 2025; Yuksekgonul
et al., 2026). Yet a completed discovery process inherently records a structured tree of exploration
decisions and their realized code-execution outcomes. By analogy to model-based reinforcement
learning and World Models (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2023) (§2), this discovery
history can serve as a replay simulator1 . Figure 2 shows an alternative strategy can navigate this
pre-recorded tree, traversing different subsets of branches in different orders, with different parallel
groupings and stopping decisions. Since all execution results are locally stored, evaluating a new
strategy requires only reading past records. This turns meta-policy improvement from expensive
online trial-and-error into a fast, simulation-based “dreaming” procedure.
Building on this insight, we introduce Dream-RSI, a framework for scalable, recursively selfimproving meta-exploration in agent-driven discovery. We first make exploration programmable
through a lightweight orchestration layer that controls branching, parallelism, and stopping while
leaving the underlying base agent unchanged. To improve this policy, Dream-RSI follows a selfimprovement loop (Figure 1): (1) Online Exploration, where the current policy drives real-world
discovery and logs execution traces; (2) Replay Simulator Construction, where recorded discovery
trees become a reusable replay simulator pool; and (3) Dreaming-based Policy Improvement,
where candidate policies are cheaply evaluated by dreaming over the simulator. The updated policy
is redeployed online, generating new experience that expands the simulator pool and closing an RSI
loop at the meta-exploration layer. We evaluate Dream-RSI on 9 discovery tasks across four domains.

2. Motivation: Discovery History as a Replay Simulator
Consider an agent navigating an unfamiliar environment. On its first attempt it wanders, hits dead
ends, and backtracks — but in doing so it builds a map. That map is reusable: a new policy can plan
over it, skip known dead ends, revisit earlier choices, and compare routes without physically walking
them again (Gupta et al., 2017). This is the logic of model-based reinforcement learning (M. Moerland
et al., 2023; Sutton, 1990). A model of how the environment responds to actions lets policies be
trained or evaluated in simulation instead of through repeated real interaction (Ha and Schmidhuber,
2018). The Dreamer family (Hafner et al., 2019, 2020, 2023, 2025) is the clearest example: learn a
compact dynamics model from collected experience, then improve the policy by imagining trajectories
inside it.
Long-horizon discovery has the same structure. An exploration policy decides which directions to
pursue, which candidates to refine, what to branch on in parallel, and when to stop. Running it online
leaves behind a structured history — branches explored, decisions made, costs incurred, outcomes
observed. As shown in Figure 2, we treat this history as an empirical replay simulator: a grounded

model of the part of the discovery space already seen. Inside this simulator, a different exploration
policy simply traces a different path through the recorded discovery tree, choosing other branches,
another ordering, a different degree of parallelism, or an earlier stopping point. Scoring that path
only requires revealing outcomes already stored along it; no rerun of the coding agent or evaluator is
needed. One expensive online run therefore funds many cheap evaluations of alternative strategies.

3. Dream-RSI: RSI through Evolving Worlds
As shown in Figure 1, Dream-RSI alternates between online exploration and offline “dreaming” for
policy evaluation to improve the exploration policy that allocates discovery computation. For online
rollout, the exploration policy directs a fixed discovery agent to generate a tree-structure discovery
trace, where each node in the tree is an discovery attempt and is scored by a fixed evaluator. The
discovery tree then becomes a replay world: alternative policies can be re-evaluated against stored
outcomes. In the offline dreaming phase, a fixed agent iteratively revises the code for the exploration
policy, and every revision is evaluated in all stored replay worlds. The best version is then deployed for
the next online rollout. Only the policy code changes; the models, evaluator, and execution interfaces
stay fixed.

all previous generation history for it, and its own filesystem snapshot, together with an evaluation

4. Experiments
We evaluate Dream-RSI on algorithm engineering, kernel optimization, mathematical optimization,
and harness optimization. We primarily use Gemini-3.1-Pro for scientific discovery experiments, with
additional Lasso experiments using Gemini-3.7-Flash to assess applicability across backbones. For
harness optimization, we use Claude Opus 4.8 with Claude Code. On the scientific discovery tasks,
our main controlled baseline is Recursive Fixed Exploration. Both methods use identical discovery
agents, evaluators, initialization, per-round budget limits, and the same hand-designed starting
policy. From the second round onward, Recursive Fixed Exploration keeps its policy fixed, while
Dream-RSI updates it through replay-based dreaming over accumulated discovery history. For
harness optimization, we focus on end-to-end performance and omit the fixed-policy comparison
due to its substantial cost: an additional full search is estimated to cost over $100,000. We measure
discovery cost as the cumulative number of discovery-agent calls.
4.1. Kernel Engineering
The goal of kernel engineering is to discover high-performance kernel implementations while preserving numerical correctness. We use four representative KernelBench tasks (Ouyang et al., 2025):
VGG16, LayerNorm, ConvDiv, and ConvMax. Candidates are scored by execution performance, measured as inverse runtime (1/ms), subject to correctness checks against the reference implementation.
With Gemini-3.1 Pro as the coding agent, we compare Dream-RSI against Recursive Fixed Exploration under an identical evaluation protocol and initialization, and against the strong PaceEvolve
baseline (Yan et al., 2026b), whose maximum discovery time we cap at two days for fairness.
Results. Figure 3 shows discovery trajectories as the number of generations increases. On VGG16
and LayerNorm, Dream-RSI matches final performance with 2.43× and 1.79× fewer generations. On
ConvDiv and ConvMax, under comparable budgets, it achieves 2.09× and 1.44× higher performance.
Adapting the exploration policy across recursive rounds thus improves both the efficiency and the
effectiveness of long-horizon discovery, and Dream-RSI surpasses PACEvolve in kernel performance
(Detailed PACEvolve scaling curves are provided in Appendix C.). Appendix E.2 gives the discovered
ConvDiv kernel. Rather than optimizing convolution in isolation, it restructures the operator chain
around downstream pooling and reduction. Adjacent convolution outputs are max-pooled immediately,
while width pooling and subsequent reductions run directly on register-resident values, avoiding
materialization of convolution and pooled feature maps. Linear operations, such as scaling, averaging,
and bias accumulation, are deferred to a lightweight final reduction.

4.3. Algorithm Engineering
In this task, we consider Lasso Regularization Path as our algorithm-engineering task, a fundamental
computational primitive in high-dimensional statistics that is widely used in model selection and
cross-validation across domains such as genomics and finance. We follow the benchmark setting of
SimpleTES (Ye et al., 2026), where the goal is to discover efficient implementations of the complete
Lasso regularization path while preserving numerical correctness. During discovery, we use the same
17 synthetic instances as SimpleTES, which cover diverse problem regimes in terms of dimensionality,
sparsity, feature correlation, and active-set structure. To evaluate whether the discovered algorithms
generalize beyond the search distribution, we additionally evaluate them on six held-out downstream
datasets spanning biological and non-biological domains. We compare against standard Lasso solvers
sklearn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) and glmnet (Friedman et al., 2010), as well as SimpleTES (Ye et al.,
2026). We run both controlled baseline and Dream-RSI for 5 rounds.

4.4. Harness Optimization
We further evaluate Dream-RSI on the harness optimization task, where the goal is to improve the
program surrounding a fixed language model, including how information is stored, retrieved, and
presented during agent execution. We follow the setting of Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026) and
initialize the discovery from Terminus-Kira as the seed harness. Each discovered harness is evaluated
on the full Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026) suite of 89 tasks, with two independent runs
per task. We use Claude Opus 4.8 with Claude Code as both the discovery agent and the policydevelopment agent, while harness evaluation is also performed with Claude Opus 4.8. We reproduce
Meta-Harness following its original search protocol, using Opus 4.8 for both search and evaluation.
Results. Figure 5 shows that Dream-RSI improves the harness throughout the discovery process,
increasing the full-suite score from 0.7247 to 0.8090. The discovered harness also outperforms Claude
Code (0.75) and Meta-Harness (0.79) under the same evaluation setting.

Self-Evolving Agents. A broader line of work studies agents that improve their own components
during interaction. Prior methods evolve model weights (Huang et al., 2026a,c; Li et al., 2026a), agent
harnesses (Lee et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b), contexts (Zhang et al., 2026d), skills (Ouyang et al.,
2026a; Wu et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026a), model behavior through test-time learning (Wang
et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026a; Yan et al., 2026a; Yuksekgonul et al., 2026), rubrics (Xiong et al.,
2026), environments (Huang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b,c) and other applications (Dai et al., 2026).
Most operate at the object level, improving components used for task execution or reasoning. Recent
work has begun to optimize meta-level mechanisms, including search strategies and self-improvement
procedures (Kim et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026a; Zhang et al.,
2026c). However, such meta-level strategies are difficult to improve because their quality is often
revealed only after costly long-horizon rollouts. Dream-RSI turns accumulated discovery history
into replay simulators for off-policy evaluation and refinement of exploration policies, then redeploys
the selected policy for new online discovery, closing a recursive self-improvement loop.
Memory, History, and Experience Reuse. Prior work reuses agent experience as search history,
context, memory, reusable skills, or training signals. DeltaEvolve structures evolutionary history
through semantic deltas (Jiang et al., 2026); SwarmResearch and MLEvolve use cross-branch or
retrospective information to guide subsequent search (Du et al., 2026; Virk et al., 2026); and other
work improves how agents access and retain experience through evolving contexts, broader harness
state, libraries, or skills (Lee et al., 2026; Ouyang et al., 2026a; Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026d).
We take a different view: rather than using exploration history only as context or memory for the
next decision, we organize it as a replay simulator in which many alternative exploration controllers
can be evaluated cheaply. This turns previously collected discovery experience into reusable feedback
for meta-level optimization, alleviating the scarcity and high cost of training signals.

7. Conclusion
We presented Dream-RSI, a framework for the recursive self-improvement of exploration policies in
long-horizon discovery. The central idea in Dream-RSI is to convert accumulated discovery history
from static context into an active, replayable simulator, directly targeting the core bottleneck of metaoptimization: delayed and expensive feedback. By "dreaming" within replay simulators constructed
from historical discovery trees, Dream-RSI evaluates candidate exploration policies rapidly and at
negligible execution cost. Improved policies are then redeployed online, driving further discovery
and expanding the simulator pool — closing the recursive self-improvement loop. Our experiments
demonstrate competitive discovery quality across diverse domains and improved discovery-agent-call
efficiency in several settings.

A. AI Use Statement
In this work, we used generative AI tools as the API in the experiments, as described in the paper: we
use Gemini 3.1 Pro in the Section 4.1,4.2,4.3, Gemini 3.7 Flash in Section 4.3 and Claude Opus 4.8

work, including text, claims or artifacts produced with the aid of generative AI.

B. Detailed Experimental Settings
B.1. Evaluation Environments
Lasso regularization-path discovery. We evaluate candidate solvers in a KVM virtual machine
with an AMD EPYC 7B12 processor, 8 vCPUs, and 32 GB RAM, running Debian-based Linux. The
evaluation protocol follows Ye et al. (2026).
GPU kernel engineering. We evaluate candidate kernels on an NVIDIA L40S GPU. Numerical
correctness is checked against the reference PyTorch implementation following the official KernelBench
