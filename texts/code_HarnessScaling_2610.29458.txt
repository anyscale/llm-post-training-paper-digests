Abstract
Agent harnesses have become a central component of modern agentic systems, shaping how models use
tools, manage context, and coordinate long-horizon interaction. Realizing the benefits of these harnesses
depends on how effectively the underlying model can make use of them, motivating reinforcement
learning directly through the harness. However, existing approaches typically train through a single fixed
harness, which can lead to specialization and limited transfer across harness configurations. To address
this, we introduce Harness Scaling, a new scaling dimension for agentic RL that extends training from a
single harness to diverse execution configurations. Concretely, we construct a factorized harness space,
select a compact training subset with balanced factorial coverage, and organize cross-harness training to
combine global coverage with controlled variation for the same task. Across multiple benchmarks, model
scales, and RL algorithms, Harness Scaling consistently improves generalization to unseen white-box
configurations. Moreover, models trained through structured white-box variation transfer effectively to
unseen external black-box harnesses, outperforming models trained through a single complex black-box
harness. These results establish harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL and
highlight the importance of how this diversity is organized for cross-harness generalization.

1. Introduction
Agent harnesses are playing an increasingly central role in modern agentic systems [31]. By supporting
sustained interaction, tool use, state maintenance, and recovery from failures [4], harnesses have substantially improved agent performance on complex, long-horizon tasks [16, 32, 38]. Yet the benefits of a
capable harness depend not only on its design, but also on whether the underlying policy can use it effectively [14]. This naturally motivates optimizing the policy directly through the harness with reinforcement
learning [36, 39], which strengthens its ability to exploit the available interaction mechanisms during task
solving.
However, existing approaches typically optimize the policy through a single fixed harness [7, 34]. While
effective within the training harness, such optimization may encourage specialization to a particular
execution configuration and weaken transfer across harnesses. We therefore introduce Harness Scaling,
extending policy optimization from a single harness to diverse execution configurations. Unlike task
or environment scaling [3, 6, 29, 35], which expands what agents solve or where they operate, Harness
Scaling introduces diversity in how policies execute and interact with the same underlying task and
environment, promoting generalization across execution configurations.
A key consideration in Harness Scaling is how to model harness variation and select a compact set of
configurations for training. Given the modular nature of agent harnesses [7, 23], we factorize harness

1

variation along five components: system prompt, tool interface, observation, context management, and
workflow. Each component contains multiple realizations, whose combinations define a factorial space of
complete harness configurations. As variation along these components increases, the space expands rapidly,
while exhaustive training can introduce substantial redundancy under a fixed budget. We therefore preserve
low-order factorial structure in a compact training set, balancing individual component realizations and
their pairwise combinations through an orthogonal design [8, 21]. During reinforcement learning, the
same task is paired with structurally nearby harness configurations, providing controlled local variation
within each task while maintaining broad coverage across the training space.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of Harness Scaling, we evaluate cross-harness generalization across
multiple benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms. By training across diverse harness configurations,
Harness Scaling reduces specialization to any single harness and encourages more generalizable agent
behavior. Empirically, it consistently outperforms single-harness RL on unseen white-box configurations.
More importantly, policies trained through structured white-box harness variation transfer effectively to
complex external black-box harnesses [1, 18], outperforming single-harness black-box training. These
results establish harness diversity as a meaningful scaling dimension for agentic RL, while showing that
effective scaling depends critically on how this diversity is organized during training. In summary, our
contributions are threefold:
• We introduce Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension for agentic reinforcement learning,
distinct from conventional scaling over tasks and environments, and study how training across
diverse harness configurations affects generalization beyond a single execution setting.
• We develop a structured framework for Harness Scaling that factorizes harness variation, selects
a compact training set with balanced factorial coverage, and organizes cross-harness training by
assigning structurally nearby configurations to the same task across the training space.
• We demonstrate across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms that Harness Scaling substantially improves generalization to unseen white-box and external black-box harnesses, establishing
both the value of scaling across harnesses and the importance of organizing this scaling structurally.

2. Related Work
2.1. Agentic Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement learning has emerged as an important paradigm for improving the capabilities of LLMs [24,
40]. Recent work has extended RL from single-turn reasoning to increasingly complex agentic settings,
including tool use [2, 26], web navigation [20], software engineering [28], and professional workflows [33].
As agentic RL moves toward broader capability, recent efforts have increasingly scaled training along the
task and environment dimensions. Task scaling [3, 5, 30] broadens the distribution of problems used for
policy optimization, while environment scaling [6, 29, 35] expands the range of external settings in which
agents interact and receive feedback. These directions expand what agents learn to solve and where they
learn to operate, while often retaining a fixed execution harness. In contrast, we study harness diversity as
an additional scaling dimension for agentic RL, focusing on policy learning and generalization across
execution configurations.
2.2. Harness Design and Optimization
The design of an agent harness can substantially shape how a language model uses tools, maintains
context, and performs long-horizon tasks [14, 16, 38]. Early agent systems largely relied on manually
designed harnesses, while subsequent work has explored automated harness improvement through search,
editing, and evolution based on task outcomes or execution trajectories [11, 13, 23]. A complementary
line of work directly optimizes model policies within complex agent harnesses, including reinforcement
learning through black-box harnesses [7, 9, 15, 17, 27, 36]. These directions either improve the harness
itself or adapt a policy to a particular harness. Harness Scaling instead studies policy optimization over a

3. Method
3.1. Problem Formulation
To study harness scaling systematically, we first formalize the space of execution configurations and
the generalization problem defined over it. We construct a factorized harness space, define a structural
notion of proximity among its configurations, and formulate the generalization objectives that motivate
the structured harness selection and multi-harness training procedures shown as in Figure 1.
3.1.1. Factorized Harness Space
A harness serves as the execution layer through which a policy interacts with its environment. Rather
than treating it as a monolithic implementation, we factorize the harness into five components: system
prompt, tool interface, observation, context management, and workflow, denoted by 𝑆, 𝑇 , 𝑂, 𝐶 , and 𝑊 ,
respectively. These components govern the instructions provided to the policy, tool access, environment
feedback, interaction history, and execution procedure, collectively shaping how the policy perceives, acts,
and maintains state throughout task execution. This factorization provides an operational decomposition
of the harness, while allowing the effects of different components to interact.

3.1.2. Generalization Objective
The central objective of Harness Scaling is to learn policies that remain effective beyond the harness
configurations observed during training. We consider two complementary forms of cross-harness generalization: broad combinatorial generalization within the constructed harness space and transfer to complex
real-world black-box harnesses beyond it.
In-Space Combinatorial Generalization. Let Htr ⊂ H denote the harness configurations used for
training. We evaluate on held-out configurations in H \ Htr , whose complete combinations are unseen
during training. This setting provides broad coverage of unseen combinations within the controlled
factorized space.
Out-of-Space Generalization. We further evaluate transfer to complex real-world black-box harnesses
whose execution configurations are not represented in H . This setting examines whether training over
structured white-box harness variation can transfer to external execution systems beyond the constructed
design space.
3.2. Harness Scaling with Structured Selection
As the factorized harness space grows combinatorially with its component dimensions, exhaustive training
can introduce substantial redundancy. We therefore construct a compact training set that preserves broad
factorial coverage under a fixed optimization budget.
3.2.1. Factorial Coverage
The harness space in Eq. 2 can be viewed as a factorial design, where each harness component corresponds
to a factor and its realizations correspond to different levels [8, 21]. To construct a compact subset
without losing basic coverage of this combinatorial space, we preserve its low-order factorial structure: the
representation of individual component realizations and their pairwise combinations. These correspond to
the first- and second-order projections of the full factorial space, respectively.
Component Balance. We first require the realizations of each component to be represented evenly
across the selected configurations. This ensures coverage along every component dimension without
overrepresenting particular realizations.
Pairwise Combination Balance. Component balance alone does not control how realizations from different components are combined. We therefore additionally balance all pairwise combinations. Indexing
the five component dimensions by 𝑖, 𝑗 ∈ {1, . . . , 5}, let 𝑁𝑖 𝑗 ( ℓ, ℓ′ ) denote the number of selected configurations containing the ℓ-th realization of component 𝑖 together with the ℓ′ -th realization of component 𝑗.

We construct the training harness set using a strength-2 orthogonal design over the component realizations

3.3. Reinforcement Learning across Harnesses
Training each task under multiple harnesses exposes the policy to different execution configurations for
the same underlying problem, encouraging the learned behavior to generalize beyond a single harness.
We realize this over the selected harness set Htr by pairing each task with a small group of structurally
related configurations. The assignment is designed to combine controlled variation within each task with
broad coverage across the training harness space, and the resulting task–harness instances form the basis
for rollout collection and policy optimization.
3.3.1. Cross-Harness Task Assignment
We use a locality-aware strategy to organize how harness configurations are assigned to tasks. For each
task 𝑥 , we assign 𝐾 configurations from Htr . An anchor harness ℎ𝑥 ∼ Unif(Htr ) is first sampled uniformly
from the training set, and the remaining 𝐾 − 1 configurations are chosen from Htr \ { ℎ𝑥 } as those with the
smallest Hamming distance 𝑑H ( ℎ𝑥 , ℎ) to the anchor. This associates each task with a small set of nearby
configurations while allowing different tasks to cover different regions of the training harness space. The
resulting harness set for task 𝑥 is

4. Experiments
In this section, we first detail the experimental setup and then report the results and detailed analysis.
4.1. Experiment Settings
Harness Scaling Setup. We instantiate five harness components with three realizations each, yielding
35 = 243 configurations in the full harness space and forming the Hamming graph 𝐻 (5, 3). Following the
strength-2 orthogonal-design construction in Section 3.2.2, the resulting design contains 18 configurations,
which form the training harness set Htr .
Training. We conduct experiments using two backbone models, Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-30BA3B-Instruct [37], on ClawGym-Task [3], which contains 13.5K workspace-based general agent tasks.
We consider both GRPO [24] and PPO [22]. For GRPO, each step uses 16 tasks with 8 rollouts per task,
while PPO uses 128 tasks with one rollout per task. We set 𝐾 = 2 harnesses per task and train for 300
optimization steps. Additional implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix C.1.
Evaluation. We evaluate on three benchmarks with varying domains and difficulty levels. ClawGymBench [3] covers workspace-based general agent tasks, PinchBench [19] focuses on realistic tasks
involving research, coding, scheduling, and file operations, and JobBench-Easy [12] evaluates more
challenging professional workflows over heterogeneous workspace files. All evaluation tasks are disjoint
from the ClawGym-Task training set. For in-space generalization, we evaluate on 18 fixed held-out
configurations from H \ Htr , selected for balanced component coverage and diversity across complete
harness configurations. The same held-out set is used across all benchmarks, model scales, and RL
algorithms. For out-of-space generalization, we use Claude Code [1] as the main external black-box
harness and additionally evaluate on OpenClaw [18]. We report Pass@1 averaged over three independent
evaluation runs, with in-space scores further averaged across the 18 held-out configurations. Further
evaluation details and OpenClaw results are provided in Appendix C.2.

settings. This suggests that repeated task exposure alone does not fully account for the improvement
from 𝐾 = 2, and that varying the harness for the same task provides additional benefit. Increasing 𝐾 to 4
provides no further improvement, with 𝐾 = 2 achieving the best overall performance among the evaluated
settings. Detailed results are provided in Appendix C.3.
• Locality-Aware Assignment Complements Global Coverage. Replacing locality-aware assignment with
random assignment reduces performance by 2.32/2.20 points on ClawGym-Bench and 3.04/1.24 points
on PinchBench under in-space/black-box evaluation. This pattern supports the global–local design of
Harness Scaling, where random anchor sampling maintains broad coverage across the training harness
space, while nearest-harness assignment provides controlled local variation for each task.

The largest gains appear in state tracking, workflow compliance, and error recovery, indicating better

These results suggest that training across harnesses particularly strengthens capabilities needed to sustain
effective execution as the surrounding harness changes.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we study Harness Scaling as a new scaling dimension for agentic reinforcement learning,
motivated by the limitations of optimizing policies through a single fixed harness. We formalize harness
diversity as a structured configuration space and develop a framework that integrates structured harness
selection with cross-harness policy training. Across benchmarks, model scales, and RL algorithms,
Harness Scaling substantially improves generalization to unseen white-box configurations and transfers
effectively to complex external black-box harnesses. Our results further show that the benefits of scaling
depend on how harness diversity is selected and incorporated into training, rather than on diversity alone.
More broadly, these findings expand the scope of agentic RL scaling beyond what agents solve and where
they operate to how they interact and execute, positioning harness variation as a complementary dimension
for improving generalization across diverse execution systems.

A. Harness Component Variants
We instantiate the factorized harness space through a model-driven transformation procedure. Starting
from the base harness, Opus-4.7 generates alternative realizations for each of the five components:
system prompt (𝑆), tool interface (𝑇 ), observation (𝑂), context management (𝐶 ), and workflow (𝑊 ). Each
transformation targets a single component and introduces a specified form of variation while preserving
the underlying task, environment, and functionality of the remaining harness components.
The transformation procedure explicitly constrains non-target components to remain unchanged in task
semantics, environment behavior, and functionality, so that each resulting realization primarily reflects
variation along the designated component dimension.
This procedure yields three realizations per component and 35 = 243 complete harness configurations.
Table 5 summarizes the resulting variants.
System Prompt (𝑆). This dimension varies how the same operational requirements are presented to
the policy. The transformed realizations range from direct instructions to product-oriented and narrative
formulations while preserving the underlying execution requirements.
Tool Interface (𝑇 ). The base interface provides five functional tool families: file reading/writing/editing,
Bash execution, code interpretation, search, and web fetching. The transformation varies how these tools
are exposed while preserving their underlying functionality. Semantic aliases retain partial cues to tool
15

usage, whereas opaque identifiers substantially reduce such cues; distractor tools further increase interface
complexity.
Observation (𝑂). This dimension varies the representation of tool feedback. Simulated infrastructure or
structured execution metadata is introduced around the original output, while the task-relevant tool result
remains unchanged.
Context Management (𝐶 ). This dimension varies how interaction history remains accessible during
long-horizon execution, ranging from full retention to in-context summarization and more aggressive
externalized compaction. Further details on how compaction participates in policy training are provided
in Appendix C.
Workflow (𝑊 ). This dimension varies the procedural structure of task execution, ranging from free-form
interaction to explicit planning and periodic progress verification while preserving the underlying task and
available actions.

B. Additional Details on Structured Harness Selection
B.1. Orthogonal Design and Coverage
We provide additional details on the structured harness selection in Section 3.2.2. Recall that the harness
space contains 𝑀 component dimensions, each with 𝐿 realizations. We construct the training harness
set using a strength-2 orthogonal design, under which every realization pair from any two component
dimensions occurs equally often.
Proposition B.1 (Coverage of a Strength-2 Orthogonal Design). Consider a strength-2 orthogonal design
over 𝑀 component dimensions, each with 𝐿 realizations. If every pairwise realization combination occurs
exactly 𝜆 times, then every realization of each component appears exactly 𝜆 𝐿 times, and the total number
of selected configurations satisfies
𝑁 = 𝜆 𝐿2 .
(8)
Proof. Consider any two component dimensions. By the strength-2 property, each of their 𝐿2 realization
pairs occurs exactly 𝜆 times. Fixing one realization of the first component and summing over all 𝐿
realizations of the second shows that the fixed realization appears exactly 𝜆 𝐿 times. Therefore, every
realization within each component is represented equally often.
Moreover, the 𝐿2 realization pairs between any two components partition the selected configurations.
Since each pair occurs 𝜆 times, the total number of configurations is
𝑁 = 𝜆 𝐿2 .

□

𝑁 ≥ 1 + 𝑀 ( 𝐿 − 1).

(9)

The bound provides a lower bound on the number of configurations required for a strength-2 orthogonal
design.
In our harness space, we use 𝑀 = 5 component dimensions with 𝐿 = 3 realizations per component.

C. Implementation Details
C.1. Training
Training Configuration. We train all models with veRL [25] using asynchronous vLLM [10] rollouts on
NVIDIA H20 GPUs, using 16 GPUs for Qwen3-4B-Instruct and 32 GPUs for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct.
Table 7 summarizes the main training hyperparameters. For GRPO, each update uses 16 tasks with eight
rollouts per task, while PPO uses 128 tasks with one rollout per task. We use an actor learning rate of
1 × 10−6 , asymmetric clipping ratios of 0.2 and 0.28, and no KL or entropy regularization. Dynamic
batching is enabled throughout training. For PPO, we use GAE with 𝛾 = 𝜆 = 1; the critic masks
observation tokens from value regression, and its value head is initialized to zero.
Training with Context Compaction. Context compaction can split a single rollout into multiple
training segments. All segments originating from the same rollout share the same rollout-level reward and
participate in policy optimization, causing the number of training segments in a batch to vary dynamically.
Segment expansion does not alter the rollout-level grouping used by GRPO: for a group of eight original
rollouts, reward normalization is always computed over the eight rollout rewards, regardless of how
many segments each rollout produces. Segments inherit the group identity and normalized advantage of
their parent rollout and do not introduce additional entries when computing the group mean or standard
deviation. Policy loss is aggregated with token-level mean reduction over all valid policy-generated tokens
in the batch. Each valid token therefore contributes once to the loss regardless of how its parent rollout
is segmented, so splitting a rollout into multiple segments does not introduce additional sample-level
weight. This preserves both the rollout-level reward semantics and consistent token-level weighting while
allowing all valid post-compaction segments to participate in training.
Black-Box Baselines. For the black-box baselines, we use OpenClaw v2026.4.5 as the training harness
and reserve Claude Code v2.1.205 as an unseen external harness for evaluation. Black-Box SFT and
Black-Box RL represent two complementary adaptation strategies to the same single black-box harness:

D. Additional Analysis Details
D.1. Behavioral Metrics
We use six trajectory-level behavioral metrics to characterize harness-related capabilities beyond tasklevel performance. Instruction Following measures whether the policy correctly follows the system
instructions and execution constraints specified by the harness. Interface Handling measures whether the
policy correctly interprets and uses the available tool interface, particularly when tool names or schemas
differ from the base harness. Observation Reading measures whether the policy extracts task-relevant
information from tool feedback despite additional formatting or distracting metadata. State Tracking
measures whether the policy maintains an accurate understanding of task progress, intermediate results,
and the evolving workspace state throughout long-horizon execution. Workflow Compliance measures
whether the policy follows procedural requirements imposed by the harness, such as explicit planning or
periodic verification. Error Recovery measures whether the policy recognizes execution failures, adapts
its strategy, and continues toward task completion rather than repeating or propagating the same error.
D.2. Metric Evaluation
We evaluate the six capabilities from complete agent trajectories using GPT-5.4 as the trajectory judge.
For each model scale and each of the 18 held-out in-space harnesses, we collect 200 complete trajectories
for both Single-Harness RL and Harness Scaling on the same 200 ClawGym-Bench tasks. For each
trajectory, the judge is provided with the task instruction, harness specification, and complete interaction
history, including policy actions, tool calls, and tool observations.
Each capability is scored independently on a five-point scale. A score of 1 indicates that the corresponding
capability is largely absent or repeatedly violated; 2 indicates substantial deficiencies; 3 indicates partial
or inconsistent satisfaction; 4 indicates generally correct behavior with minor deviations; and 5 indicates
consistent and effective satisfaction of the criterion throughout the trajectory. The judge assigns each
dimension separately rather than producing a single overall trajectory score.
We use the same judging prompt, rubric, and judge model across both approaches and all harness settings.
For each behavioral dimension, we first average scores over the 200 trajectories within each held-out
harness, and then average equally across all 18 harnesses. For visualization, the five-point scores are
normalized by dividing by five, resulting in values between 0.2 and 1.0 as reported in Figures 4a and 4b.
