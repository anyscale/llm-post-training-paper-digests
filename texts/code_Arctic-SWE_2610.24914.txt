A BSTRACT
Long-horizon agent training, including software engineering, is advancing rapidly,
but the recipe and infrastructure needed for a strong reinforcement-learning baseline remain difficult to reproduce. This makes gains from new algorithms or data
hard to distinguish from improvements to an under-tuned baseline. We present
Arctic-SWE, a fully asynchronous RL framework that establishes such a baseline
through model–system co-design, using overfitting on a small task pool as the
testbed. This testbed exposes failures that early reward gains conceal and guides
the integration of established components: gated reward, importance-weight control, aligned training and inference engines, and consistent state across policy updates. No single component is new, yet together they form the missing foundation.
On raw R2E-Gym data, without new algorithms or data work, Arctic-SWE trains
Qwen3.5-4B to 62.0% pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified. This is comparable to
concurrent FrogNano (61.5%), which relies on online task synthesis, after about
one-sixth as many training rollouts. Building on this foundation, we further filter and refine R2E-Gym, SWE-rebench V2, and Scale-SWE. With this curated
subset, the 4B model improves from 47.4%/25.0%/37.9% to 65.0%/38.3%/59.7%
on SWE-bench Verified/Pro-v1/Pro-v2, and the recipe also improves Qwen3.59B from 56.2%/31.9%/48.8% to 65.7%/39.6%/70.2% on Verified/Pro-v1/Pro-v2.
Controlled comparisons show that both curation and test strengthening improve
held-out performance, although stronger tests reduce apparent training-task mastery. Arctic-SWE provides a documented starting point for studying better algorithms, data and environments for asynchronous agent RL.

I NTRODUCTION

Long-horizon agents learn to solve tasks through extended interaction with tools and environments.
Software engineering is a prominent example: agents inspect repositories, edit code and execute
tests, with reinforcement learning (RL) improving their ability to complete these workflows (Pan
et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2025; Agentica & Together AI, 2025). Recent work advances both online task
synthesis, as in FrogNano (Kim et al., 2026), and large-scale model–system co-design, as in MiMoV2.6 (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026). Yet reproducing a strong training baseline requires choices that
a named algorithm or a released checkpoint does not specify. For academic and open-source efforts
with limited compute, establishing that baseline is itself a substantial research problem.
This matters when assessing new methods. An algorithm or dataset can appear better because the
comparison recipe is under-tuned, much as optimizer comparisons depend on tuning AdamW or
SGD with momentum (Wen et al., 2025), and policy-gradient comparisons depend on implementation details (Engstrom et al., 2020). In supervised fine-tuning, establishing a baseline is straightforward: minimizing cross-entropy reliably overfits a small training set, which provides a basic
implementation check. Group-relative RL methods such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) have no com1

parably routine baseline check for long, stateful agent interactions. Early progress is insufficient: a
recipe can learn tasks and subsequently lose them.
Asynchrony makes the baseline a joint modeling and systems problem. Generation, tool execution,
and optimization must overlap heavily to maintain throughput, so the system efficiency now directly
changes the age of the experience used for learning. Weight refreshes can leave cached states inconsistent, and different numerical paths can assign different probabilities to the same tokens. Grading
and termination further determine which trajectories receive credit. These system-level choices directly alter the data reaching the optimizer, not only its throughput.
We present Arctic-SWE, a fully asynchronous RL framework built on Prime-RL (PrimeIntellect,
2026a). We use overfitting as a stress test to establish its recipe: prolonged training on a small
pool checks whether the pipeline both acquires and sustains success. The failures guide feedback,
optimizer and execution changes (Section 3). None of these changes is new on its own; what has
been missing is the foundation they form together. On the existing R2E-Gym (Jain et al., 2025)
dataset, this foundation alone yields 62.0% SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2023) pass@1
for Qwen3.5-4B, without new data construction or refinement. FrogNano centers its contribution
on online task synthesis and reports 61.5% after consuming about 6 times more training rollouts
compared to ours. A solid recipe, rather than elaborate data or algorithm work, already reaches this
level.
A stronger baseline also lets us ask what better data contributes. Execution-grounded filtering
and issue refinement retain a subset of the source pool combined from R2E-Gym, SWE-rebench
V2 (Badertdinov et al., 2026), and Scale-SWE (Zhao et al., 2026). Matched comparisons show that
this curation improves generalization; strengthening weak tests recovers additional useful tasks and
improves learning despite reducing apparent mastery (Section 4). The resulting framework and curated training data improve both 4B and 9B models on SWE-bench Verified/Pro-v1/Pro-v2 (Deng
et al., 2025) from 47.4%/25.0%/37.9% to 65.0%/38.3%/59.7%, and from 56.2%/31.9%/48.8% to
65.7%/39.6%/70.2%, respectively. These full-scale runs yield further insights into the recipe (Section 5). Our aim is to make a strong baseline reproducible, so that further advances in algorithms,
data and environments can be measured against a training pipeline that already learns effectively.

S ETUP

All experiments share the following pipeline; Appendix A gives the full specification.
Hardware and pipeline. All training runs use one node of eight B200 GPUs: two train the policy
and six serve it as independent vLLM inference engines. For asynchronous training, the inference
engines keep generating while the trainer updates and receive new weights after every update, so one
multi-turn rollout can span several policy versions. The max_off_policy_steps parameter
controls such staleness: a rollout more than that many policy versions stale is dropped.
Model and agent. We train Qwen3.5-4B in all sections, and we train Qwen3.5-9B with our final full
recipe in Section 5. Each rollout runs our Mini-SWE-Agent-Plus (Kwai-Klear, 2026) harness, which
has two tools execute_bash and edit_via_str_replace, in its own sandbox container on
the training node. A trajectory is capped at 100 turns and 131,072 tokens in total; each model
response is capped at 32,768 tokens. Training rollouts sample at temperature 1.0 on full vocabulary;
evaluation samples at temperature 1.0 with top-p 0.95.
Training and evaluation. Each update samples a group of rollouts for every task in the batch. We
optimize with CISPO (MiniMax, 2025), which weights each token’s gradient by its importance ratio
between the trainer and the rollout policy; the ratio is detached and bounded at 2.0. We evaluate
on SWE-bench Verified (a 483-problem subset) and SWE-bench Pro with the same harness and
budgets, and report pass@1 estimated via the mean success over four rollouts per problem.

3.1

RL OVERFITTING AS THE S TRESS T EST

If an RL pipeline cannot reliably memorize a small, solvable subset of tasks, it cannot be expected
to generalize over a large, noisy corpus. To isolate infrastructure failures from genuine algorithmic
limits, we use overfitting as a diagnostic stress test. We filter 160 tasks from R2E-Gym where
Qwen3.5-4B passes the unit tests in at least one out of four attempts, and train on them in our
Mini-SWE-Agent-Plus harness (Appendix A) with 32 tasks per update and five rollouts per task.
Alongside reward, we track token entropy and rollout–trainer mismatch KL, the mean of ω−log ω−1
over trainable tokens, where ω is the trainer probability of a sampled token divided by its recorded
rollout probability (see formal definition in Appendix B.2).
A promising early curve can conceal a failing recipe. To illustrate this, we test two established
public baselines on our 160-task overfit set (Figure 1). We evaluate a synchronous pipeline (SkyRL,
SkyRL Contributors (2026)), which pauses the trainer to generate all rollouts using the exact current policy, alongside an asynchronous pipeline (Prime-RL, PrimeIntellect (2026a)), which overlaps
generation and optimization to maximize throughput but introduces policy lag. When adapted to
Qwen3.5-4B (see details in Appendix B.2), both recipes initially appear successful, climbing to
roughly 70% reward (Figure 1a). However, prolonged training exposes severe instabilities especially in async training. Neither recipe reliably sustains its early gains. This shows that standard
algorithms are remarkably brittle over long horizons, and that evaluating only the first few epochs
of a training run obscures fatal recipe deficits.
Why the diagnostic matters beyond the overfitting tasks. Standard agent training rarely runs for
dozens of epochs on a small dataset, but the instability that prolonged overfitting exposes still limits
full-scale training. If a recipe acquires task behaviors at rate g and loses them at rate d, its mastery
of solvable tasks stays below 1 − ε with ε = d/(g + d); on a full dataset in which such tasks make
up a fraction ρ, reward then stays at least ρε below the attainable optimum (Appendix B.1). A leaky
recipe can show rapid early gains yet carry a permanent ceiling that neither more data nor a larger
model removes, so the feedback loop must be stabilized first.
3.2

C ORRECTING A LGORITHMIC F EEDBACK

Having established that standard recipes are unstable in prolonged training, we repair the training
loop one layer at a time, starting with the algorithmic feedback: when does each rollout terminate,
and which/when rollouts earn reward. We read each change from the earlier-stack curves of Figure 1;
Section 3.3 revisits them after the system fix.
Do not let harness remedies pass unchecked into reward. Agent harnesses often help a model
past a malformed turn, for instance by returning a syntactic hint and letting the rollout continue. If
the rollout then succeeds, the invalid prefix shares in the positive trajectory reward, so the policy
is reinforced for behavior the harness corrected rather than behavior it produced. The Prime-RL
recipe leaves what the harness does unconstrained, so hinted invalid turns go unpenalized; its line in
Figure 1 collapses by epoch 11.

explicit submission and terminate duplicate tool calls, invalid formats and exhausted turn or token

Check the raw token output, not only the parsed call. The gate above inspects tool calls after
the tool-call parser has converted the sampled tokens into a structured call. Parsers are lenient,
so a malformed raw token output that the parser repairs can still earn reward and get reinforced
over time. On the train-time paths of major public RL frameworks (PrimeIntellect, 2026a; SkyRL

check line also validates the raw token output itself: the reasoning block, the tool-call envelope and
the tags inside each call. It never collapses but still falls short of a full fit: reward climbs slowly,
holding around 80% and peaking at 87.3%.
3.3

C ORRECTING THE S YSTEM L AYER

An algorithmically sound update can still diverge if the experience supplied by the asynchronous
rollout engines is inconsistent with the policy being trained. Unlike synchronous RL, where system
efficiency affects only training speed, asynchronous RL lets the speed of the rollout engines and the
trainer shape the training dynamics as well.
Match training and inference numerics. Qwen3.5’s hybrid recurrent architecture makes its repeatedly updated state particularly sensitive to rounding errors. By aligning the numerical paths between the rollout generators and the trainer, we reduced rollout–trainer mismatch KL from roughly
3 × 10−4 to 1.2 × 10−4 without a throughput penalty (default versus numerically aligned in Figure 2a,b). Specifically, across both optimization and weight transfer, we retain FP32 for recurrent
states, gate arithmetic, and Alog , the learned per-head decay rate of the recurrent state in log space
(Appendix C.1). Other FP32 paths, such as master weights, gradient reduction, RMSNorm statistics
and the LM head, are now standard practice and not specific to our recipe.
Reconstruct state after a weight refresh. When active model weights are updated asynchronously,
cached historical states (attention KV, convolution history and recurrent state) immediately misalign
with the new policy. PipelineRL (Piché et al., 2025) reuses stale KV caches, having found that
they barely increase the divergence from the on-policy distribution for a softmax-attention model on
single-turn reasoning. Multi-turn agent rollouts can span several weight refreshes, and in our hybrid
model, stale attention and GatedDeltaNet caches both shift next-token distributions (Appendix C.3).
We therefore invalidate all caches on refresh and re-prefill the retained history (KV recomputation).
Schedule for useful, recent experience. A policy-lag cap alone does not control the age distribution
of the batch. Six vLLM engines serve rollouts (Appendix A), and a router assigns requests by
session hashing, pinning each trajectory to one engine to reuse its prefix cache. This ignores load:
one engine held 186 concurrent requests against 8 on the others. Because a group is trained only
once all its rollouts complete, these stragglers pushed whole groups past the off-policy cap of four
updates. Our dispatcher instead assigns each group to the least-loaded engine and caps resident

AUDITING AND REPAIRING THE TRAINING REWARD THROUGH EXECUTION

Section 3 achieves 62.0% on SWE-bench Verified with R2E-Gym tasks checked only for executability. But a good recipe cannot fix a bad reward from fragile data, and that check is weak. On nearly
a third of the tasks that pass it, a plausible wrong patch still obtains full reward. Standard data
validation often misses this, because it typically does not score a wrong patch. This section filters
and repairs the tasks themselves without making any: we draw on three existing released datasets.
Section 4.1 checks each task’s three artifacts against one another, keeping 7,500 of 21,099 and
rewriting the issue where it is at fault; Section 4.2 strengthens the tests of the largest rejected category instead of discarding it. Both are validated by controlled experiments in which only the training
data changes.
4.1

W HEN THE ISSUE , THE TESTS AND THE GOLD PATCH DISAGREE

Every SWE training task is three artifacts that are supposed to describe the same change: (1) the
issue the agent reads, (2) the test suite that computes its reward, and (3) the gold patch that defines the
intended fix. Nothing in how opensourced datasets are built guarantees the three agree. The fail-topass gate that every existing dataset applies (Yang et al., 2025a; Jain et al., 2025; Badertdinov et al.,
2026) checks only the weakest consequence of agreement—that the tests separate the buggy tree
from gold—and never asks whether they also accept patches that do not fix the issue, nor whether
the issue states what they demand. Our curation therefore runs in two pipelines. Pipeline 1 keeps
that gate and applies it with code execution only: over 21,099 candidates from R2E-Gym, SWErebench-V2, and Scale-SWE (Jain et al., 2025; Badertdinov et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), it runs
the graded suite on the buggy and gold trees to confirm the fail-to-pass behavior and instruments
every assertion to identify which are binding, admitting 17,861 (Table 1).
Clearing that gate does not make the three artifacts agree. A pull-request test is written by someone
who already holds the fix, so it is usually a regression test rather than a specification built to reject
wrong patches; and an issue written before the fix often says less than the tests come to demand.
Both gaps are invisible to the Pipeline-1 gate, and to any check that reads the three artifacts without
scoring a patch against them. The audit described next finds that on 5,351 of the 17,861, nearly a
third, a plausible wrong patch written from the issue alone still earns full reward (too_loose), and
that on 1,233 the tests demand behavior the issue never mentions (issue_underspecified).
Left alone, both could create misaligned training signals: a loose test suite assigns full reward to
some issue-inconsistent patches, creating opportunities for reward hacking as RL training explores
a broader patch distribution, while an underspecified issue can penalize behavior that cannot be
inferred from the prompt (Appendix E.6).
Pipeline 2 answers the two questions the Pipeline-1 gate never asks with the help of LLMs. It gives
an agent powered by Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026) the gold patch, the tests, the Pipeline-1
measurements, and a shell, and puts three questions to it in a fixed dependency order: (1) whether
the gold patch fixes the issue, (2) whether the tests grade what the issue asks, and—only if both
hold—(3) whether the issue states what the tests demand. It is privileged but not trusted to answer
by inspection: the middle question is settled by writing counterfactual patches and scoring them with
the training scorer used in RL, the agent may only rewrite the issue text. Pipeline 2 accepts 7,500
of the 17,861: 6,267 with the issue unchanged and 1,233 rewritten to state what the tests already
grade (Appendices E and F).

Experimental validation. Curation is tested by a controlled experiment in which only the training
data changes, with both arms scored by pass@1 on held-out SWE-bench Verified (Figure 5a,b). The
control is size- and mixture-matched: 7,500 rows that clear pipeline 1 but are rejected by pipeline 2,
kept in their original non-rewritten form, so the comparison isolates exactly what pipeline 2 adds
on top of the standard gate (Appendix E.7). The curated deck leads at all five matched steps. It
also learns its deck faster, retiring 35.3% of its rows as mastered by step 250 against 23.8% for
the control—a training-side quantity, and the one that explains the evaluation gap. Curation therefore does not just remove work: the control keeps spending rollouts on tasks it can probably never
master—possibly because of an underspecified issue or a mis-specified grading criterion—while the
curated arm converts the same budget into solved tasks that are then evicted, so more of the compute
can be used on tasks that can still teach it something.
Alignment is expensive: 10,361 validated tasks are rejected, 9,379 of them on their tests rather than
their content. The largest class, too_loose, is defective only in its test grading. Section 4.2 asks
whether those rows can be recovered by repairing the tests instead of discarding the task, which also
turns the label from a model judgment into a claim a controlled experiment can test.

4.2

R EPAIRING THE TESTS : STRENGTHENING TOO - LOOSE SUITES

Repairing a defective test suite in place is what pipeline 2 did not attempt, and dropping these rows
instead is the single largest source of data loss in our pipeline. The issue, gold patch, container,
and buggy tree stay fixed while the graded tests are strengthened to reject the shortcuts that beat
them, subject to gold still passing and to every added check following from the issue or established
public behavior rather than from gold’s implementation. Strengthening suites rather than discarding
their tasks is established practice for evaluation benchmarks but untested as a training intervention
(Appendix K). The procedure recovers 4,909 of the 5,351 suites (91.7%), of which 4,889 form
paired training arms holding identical instance IDs and differing in nothing but the unit test suite
that computes reward (Appendix G).
Experimental validation. The two arms hold identical rows and differ only in the test suite that
grades them, so the comparison isolates the reward signal itself (Figure 5c,d). The strengthened arm
leads at every matched step: its step-200 score of 59.1 exceeds every raw score through step 350
(55.5), so strengthening is worth more than 150 training steps. It also masters its deck more slowly—
29.0% of rows retired by step 250 against 40.9% for raw—which is the expected consequence of
a stronger grader: a rollout that previously collected full reward without fixing the bug no longer
passes, so tasks are no longer retired on the strength of false successes. Because the rows are
identical, that performance gap is not a difficulty difference but an estimate of how much of the raw
arm’s mastery was spurious, and the arm that masters less is the one that transfers better.

T RAINING AND EVALUATION ON THE CURATED TASKS

Section 4 argued that a training task is only as good as the agreement between its issue, its tests
and its gold patch, and measured that agreement one curation step at a time. This section spends
the resulting training data on a single long run and reports what it buys on held-out evaluation. We
describe the evaluation and the sandbox it runs in before the training configuration, because the
metric and two of its constraints apply to training rollouts as well.
5.1

E VALUATION UNDER SANDBOX ISOLATION

We evaluate on SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2023) and SWE-bench Pro (Deng et al., 2025),
reporting pass@1 averaged over 4 rollouts per task. We use Verified as our validation set and use
Pro as the test set, as the latter is much slower to evaluate (Appendix H.5).
The environment those rollouts run in needs more explanation than the metric does, because an
agent with a shell in a repository has two ways to perform evaluation hacking that do not involve
solving the task: (1) the repository’s own git history, in which the gold-fix commit is a descendant of
the checked-out buggy parent and so is reachable offline by git log -all, and (2) the network,
which offers the upstream fix and arbitrary package installation. On 60 SWE-bench Pro trials (Table 2, Appendix H.6), a Qwen3.8-27B agent in the default setting hacks 19 of them, which inflates
its resolve rate from 50.0% to 65.0%. The same audit finds no hacks under our setting. Similar
to concurrent work at Cursor (Jain, 2026), we therefore prune the history when each container is
prepared and run every sandbox under an egress policy denying outbound traffic and cloud metadata
access. Training and evaluation rollouts face the same isolation.
Because isolation grades the gold patch too, a few tasks become ungradeable; excluding those and a
few unrelated defects leaves 483 SWE-bench Verified problems, fixed across runs and checkpoints,
and all 731 SWE-bench Pro problems (Appendix H). These controls lower our scores, so any
comparison against numbers reported under the default setting understates our results.
5.2

T RAINING SETUP

We train two base models, Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B, on the same curated pool of Section 4
drawn from R2E-Gym, SWE-rebench V2, and Scale-SWE, each prepared with its git history pruned
as in Section 5.1. All three training decks are screened against SWE-bench Verified to avoid contamination (Appendix F.5). The objective is the CISPO-style loss introduced in Appendix A without
KL penalty, taking 16 prompts with n=8, and no length penalty is applied. We also apply token-level
importance masking in the style of IcePop (Zhao et al., 2025) with range [0, 2.0]. Beyond the data,
three small settings differ from Section 3: we retire only mastered tasks; we override the reward of
invalid trajectories to −1 (Appendix I.3); and we disable the duplicate-tool-call detector. The 9B
run additionally updates three training settings, which Appendix I.5 states in full. Appendix I gives
the deck, budgets, and optimizer settings in full, with the objective in Appendix A and the reward
contract in Appendix I.3.

R ESULTS

Table 4 reports each checkpoint under its own sampling configuration (Appendix H.5.1). The
Qwen3.5-4B model reaches 65.0% pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified, up from 47.4% at initialization and past the 62.0% that Section 3 reached on R2E-Gym alone, and the 9B reaches 65.7%. The
gains hold on the harder benchmark: SWE-bench Pro-v1/v2 rise from 25.0/37.9 to 38.3/59.7 for the
4B and from 31.9/48.8 to 39.6/70.2 for the 9B. Because our training data contains only Python tasks,
we also report Pro-v2-Py, the Python subset of Pro-v2. The gain there, 21.5 points for the 4B and
18.4 for the 9B, is no larger than on Pro-v2 as a whole (21.8 and 21.4), so the improvement is not
confined to the language the run was trained on.
Figure 6b tracks Verified across training under a single fixed configuration (T =1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k
20) rather than the per-checkpoint settings of Table 4. There the 4B peaks at 64.2 at step 250 and
the 9B at 65.7 at step 550; over the 200 steps following its peak the 9B gives back 2.8 points, where
the 4B gives back 8.6.
That decline does not register in the 4B’s training signal. Over steps 250 to 450, as pass@1 falls
13.4% relative, the eviction-adjusted reward declines only 3.4%. That metric imputes reward 1 for
every retired task (Appendix J.1), so it is blind to the failure by construction: crediting a task that is
never re-checked assumes its competence is preserved.
We test this directly, and the assumption fails. By step 350 eviction has retired 44% of the 4B’s deck,
rising to 48% by step 450 (Figure 6c), so we re-scored a sample of the tasks retired by step 350 under
frozen weights (Table 3). At step 350 they score 0.95, close to the 1.0 they earned when they left,
so eviction had been retiring genuinely mastered tasks rather than lucky ones. The same tasks score
0.85 at step 500. The 4B is forgetting the population it no longer trains on, and the training reward
cannot see it (Appendix J).
The 9B does not. We run the same probe on a 9B trained on the 4B’s exact deck and settings,
changing only the base model (Appendix I.5). On decks cut from its own eviction state, which had
retired 41% of the deck by step 350 against the 4B’s 44%, it scores 0.97 at step 350 and 0.97 at
step 500 — a decline statistically indistinguishable from zero. Deck depletion alone is therefore not
sufficient to produce the degradation. Our hypothesis is that the Qwen3.5-4B lacks the capacity to
retain a growing mastered population while continuing to fit the residual tail.
Table 4 places the run against published results on SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Pro. The
comparison is indicative rather than controlled: the systems differ in scaffold, inference budget
and the amount of supervision behind them, and only the rows marked with † share this paper’s
evaluation harness. To make at least one comparison direct, we re-evaluated the concurrent FrogNano (Kim et al., 2026) in our harness under the sampling parameters its authors report. It scores
60.4 on Verified and 37.0 on Pro-v1, against the 61.5 and 37.6 its authors report (they do not report
Pro-v2).

D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Strong RL baselines for long-horizon agents remain hard to reproduce, so gains from new algorithms or data are hard to separate from an under-tuned baseline. We treat prolonged overfitting on
a small, solvable task pool as the routine check such a baseline has lacked: it exposes failures that
early reward gains conceal, and it guides which established components the recipe needs. In asynchronous RL these components span modeling and systems, because system efficiency, cached state
and numerical paths all change the experience the optimizer sees. Gated reward, importance-weight
control, aligned training and inference engines, and consistent state across policy updates are each
known, but together they form the missing foundation.
That foundation alone trains Qwen3.5-4B to 62.0% pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified from unchanged R2E-Gym tasks, comparable to FrogNano’s 61.5% with online task synthesis, after about
one-sixth as many training rollouts. On top of it, data work becomes measurable: curation and test
strengthening raise the 4B model to 65.0%/38.3%/59.7% and the 9B model to 65.7%/39.6%/70.2%
on SWE-bench Verified/Pro-v1/Pro-v2. Most comparisons are single-seed and use one harness, and
the Verified subset also serves for checkpoint selection. Arctic-SWE offers a documented starting
point against which future algorithms, data and environments for asynchronous agent RL can be
measured.

F ORMAT HANDLING IN PUBLIC TRAINING STACKS

Qwen3.5 closes the reasoning block opened by its generation prompt with </think> and
then calls a tool in the XML format its chat template specifies: <tool_call> wraps one
<function=NAME> block, which holds one <parameter=KEY> block per argument. We
write 13 malformed turns, each breaking the template’s format in one element of a valid
execute_bash(ls) turn, and run them through the path each stack uses to turn a sampled turn
into an executed action (Table 6). For the four RL frameworks this is the train-time rollout path
of the SWE example we cite (PrimeIntellect, 2026a; SkyRL Contributors, 2026; THUDM, 2026;
RadixArk, 2026), at the main-branch commit listed in Table 6; the cited revisions give the same
results, except that Prime-RL’s older renderer repairs a missing </think>. Prime-RL parses the
sampled token IDs with its Qwen3.5 renderer and runs its bash harness. slime and miles parse with
SGLang’s qwen3_coder and qwen3 parsers and run Claude Code and mini-swe-agent, respectively. SkyRL’s Mini-SWE example serves vLLM without a tool parser and extracts one fenced
bash block from the text, so its column tests the bash-block analogue of each flaw. The vLLM and
SGLang columns show these servers’ parsers alone, as chat-completion clients use them. The two
Arctic-SWE columns run our harness with the gated reward of Section 3.2, without and with the raw
token check.
Every public train-time path still executes a call for most of these 13 turns, and none penalizes a
flawed turn: reward comes only from the final tests. Some repairs change the action itself: with
a duplicated parameter, every public XML parser keeps the last value and runs rm -rf build
instead of ls. When no call is parsed, Prime-RL and slime end the episode and grade the repository
as it stands, while SkyRL and miles return a format message and continue. Prime-RL and miles train
the sampled tokens of every executed turn, and so does slime with Claude Code 2.1.233 and later,
so a turn the parser repaired is reinforced whenever the rollout succeeds. SkyRL instead re-renders
each turn through the chat template, which rewrites the reasoning flaws, and it also trains the turns
it rejected. Our gate alone still executes 6 of them because it inspects the parsed call; the raw token
check rejects all 13 and sets the rollout’s reward to zero. Our harness additionally rejects empty
reasoning, prose between the reasoning and the call, and more than one call per turn. The template
permits all three, so we treat them as preferences of our harness rather than format errors and leave
them out.

Other rounding sites follow the inference engine. For residual addition and RMSNorm, we
match vLLM’s execution order by normalizing the FP32 sum while storing the residual in BF16.
For query and key RoPE, BF16 rounding occurs after normalization, and the rotation itself runs in
FP32 before casting back. Both engines keep LM-head outputs in FP32, so logits are not rounded
before log-probabilities are computed. In the backward pass, selective upcasting limits memory
overhead without changing forward numerics.
Alignment lowers training-time mismatch without slowing rollout. Figure 2a,b compares the
default and aligned configurations over training steps 10–50, excluding warm-up transients. Mean
rollout–trainer mismatch KL (Equation 11) falls from about 3 × 10−4 to 1.2 × 10−4 and stays
lower throughout. Throughput is unchanged, because the upcast paths (gate nonlinearities, the decay
parameter and residual normalization) cost little next to the projection and attention kernels.

kernels and reduction orders. Our numerical work includes an fp32 sum in fused add-RMSNorm,

fp32 rotary and gate computations, and float32 recurrent cache state. We score the same recorded token sequences with identical weights on both engines, removing policy drift from this audit. Across

staleness. In particular, the fraction attributed to execution by such a ratio varies substantially with

C.2

S ERVING ADMISSION CHANGES REALIZED STALENESS

The configured bound does not determine the observed policy age. At the same configured offpolicy cap of four, a change from hash-based assignment to load-aware admission with per-client
caps coincides with lower logged depth, lower trainer/sampler divergence and shorter steps. Entropy
is similar, at 0.229 versus 0.240. This is evidence that serving controls change the conditions under
which updates are made, even when the nominal algorithm configuration is unchanged.
Per-rank placement removes a straggler engine. With one endpoint of six data-parallel ranks,
the router’s session hash assigns requests without seeing rollouts that are idle in a sandbox between
turns, and equal arrival rates diverge once a replica thrashes its prefix cache. In a cap-four run, one
replica reached 186 concurrent requests against 8 on the others, its prefix-cache hit rate fell from
96% to 57%, and buffered rollouts climbed from 143 to 660. The dispatcher instead pins each group
of eight rollouts to the rank that owns the fewest in-flight rollouts, and vLLM caps each engine at
56 resident sequences. Resuming that run from its update-200 checkpoint with this placement, over
updates 201–240:
• rollouts dropped at the off-policy cap fall from 27.0 to 0.76 per update;
• mean and 90th-percentile policy lag fall from 3.1 and 4.7 to 2.0 and 3.1 updates;
• generation throughput rises from 298k to 417k tokens/s, and waiting for a batch falls from
18.8 to 8.2 minutes per update, while forward–backward time stays at about 3 minutes;
• training-time mismatch KL falls from 2.45 × 10−4 to 1.58 × 10−4 .
Dressage separately reports load-balancing gains under synchronous rollout workloads (Dressage
Contributors, 2026a); our measurement emphasizes policy age as well as elapsed time.
Report time waiting for useful work. Trainer throughput remains between 28.1k and 30.8k tokens per second across the serving comparison because the main difference is time waiting for
rollouts. Separate trainer performance work improves compute throughput, but one instrumented
run still spends 175.6 seconds in forward/backward computation and 278.4 seconds waiting for a
batch out of a 454.0-second loop. Step time, executed work and realized sample age are therefore
needed alongside kernel throughput to explain the training budget.

E

C URATION PIPELINE : COMPOSITION , VERDICTS AND CONSTRAINTS

E.1

T HE COUNTERFACTUAL PROBE

The fail-to-pass gate evaluates a suite on exactly two patches: the empty one and gold. Passing
it certifies that the suite separates those two trees and nothing more. A suite can do that and still
accept most of the patch space between them, and reading the issue, the tests or the gold patch can
not reveal it naturally, because the question is about patches that do not yet exist. Pipeline 2’s second
question is answered by creating them.
23

Having read the issue, the gold patch and pipeline 1’s record of which assertions are binding, the
judge writes two kinds of patch and scores each with the same scorer that computes reward during
RL—the same node-ID contract, the same container, the same pass/fail aggregation—so that a probe
result is a statement about the training signal rather than about a reimplementation of it.
Plausible-but-wrong patches are written from the issue alone, of the kind a competent solver
might produce without access to gold: a shortcut that satisfies the letter of the reported
symptom, a partial fix covering the reported case but not the general one, or an alias
that makes a name resolve without performing the change the issue asks for. If such a
patch scores 1.0, the suite accepts a patch that does not fix the issue, and the verdict is
too_loose.
Correct-but-different patches resolve the issue by an implementation other than gold’s. If such a
patch scores 0.0, the suite is enforcing an implementation contract the issue never states,
and the verdict is over_restrictive.
The two probes are what make the check two-sided. A procedure that only sought weak suites
would have a standing incentive to call every suite weak, and one that only sought over-constrained
suites would reproduce the orientation of prior filters (Appendix E.6). Running both means a suite
is rejected for admitting wrong patches and for rejecting right ones, and the verdict distribution in
Table 11 shows both classes at comparable scale (30.0% and 17.6%).
Only the verdict is a model judgement. The reward figures the judge cites are produced by executing
its patches, so a mistaken verdict requires the judge to mis-describe an executed result rather than
merely to reason poorly about code it has read. Two residual limits are worth stating. First, a probe
establishes that a wrong patch passes, not that most wrong patches do; the rate we report is therefore
a lower bound on how permissive a suite is, since a judge that fails to find a passing shortcut does not
prove none exists. Second, the probe records in Appendix F are the judge’s own accounts of what
it ran, not an independent re-execution. The downstream check on whether the too_loose label
tracks something real is the controlled training comparison in Section 4.2, where suites carrying that
label are strengthened and the same instance IDs are trained both ways.
E.2

W HAT PIPELINE 1 EXCLUDES

Pipeline 1 uses no model. It runs the graded suite on the buggy and gold trees to derive (or verify)
the fail-to-pass gate, then instruments every assertion in the gating tests to identify which ones are
binding, i.e. fail on the buggy tree and pass on gold. Its output is a deterministic record of what each
suite does, and it excludes 3,238 rows in three separately recorded classes (Table 10).
First, 310 Scale-SWE rows ship without a container image and can never be executed. Second, 583
rows the harness could not measure reliably—sandbox and exec failures (193), suites that did not run
or failed collection (321), and gold trees that fail their own suite (69). We record these as failures to
measure rather than quality verdicts, because on a deck whose gold is known good a gold mismatch
is more often evidence that we are misreading the suite than that the task is broken. Third, 2,345
rows that fail validation outright: in 1,652 the reference patch does not make the declared gate pass,
391 have no usable gate at all (already passing on the buggy tree, declared tests not collected, or
none derivable), 176 are non-pytest suites where the assertion probe recorded nothing, and 126 have
a gold patch that does not apply. The dominant failure is a reference solution that does not satisfy
the tests defining its own task—a defect no downstream judge could repair.
E.3

R ESULTING COMPOSITION

The two acceptance rates in Table 10 are the clearest summary of what the second pipeline adds. The
standard fail-to-pass gate admits 84.7% of source instances; of those, only 42.0% survive the judge,
for an end-to-end rate of 35.5%. Nearly all of the filtering, and all of the filtering that concerns
whether reward is valid, happens in pipeline 2.
Table 11 breaks those rejections down by verdict, and the profile is corpus-dependent and diagnostic.
Scale-SWE, whose gate is a single human-written PR test—a weak discriminator by construction—
is rejected predominantly as too_loose (3,210 rows, 35.5% of the corpus), whereas SWErebench’s multi-test gates more often over-constrain, failing as over_restrictive (1,227)
24

slightly more often than as too_loose (1,155). Only 2 of 1,235 attempted rewrites were rejected
for eliding the original, so the rewrite pathway is almost never the binding constraint; the rejections
are overwhelmingly about test quality.
E.4

C ONSTRAINTS ON ISSUE REWRITING

When a rewrite is warranted the judge may edit the problem_statement and nothing else. It
adds the missing behavioural requirement while preserving the original’s voice and reproduction
snippet, states what must happen rather than how, and never names private helpers or file locations
introduced by the gold patch. A rewrite that elides content it claims to reproduce is rejected and the
row dropped.
E.5

W HY REWRITING IS NOT ALWAYS THE RIGHT REPAIR

Rewriting and rejection begin from the same observation—the tests grade something the issue never
mentions—and differ only in which document is at fault. When the unstated requirement is legitimate user-visible behaviour, the issue is deficient and we add the requirement (Appendix F.2). When
the test instead demands gold’s implementation, writing that contract into the issue would turn “fix
this bug” into “reproduce this implementation” and reward a test that is itself defective, so the row is
rejected as over_restrictive (17.6%, Table 11; Appendix F.3). This is why the judge’s questions are asked in a fixed order: asked in the opposite order, it would rewrite every over-restrictive
test into a well-specified task.
The verdict records support this reading of the two defects as one mismatch. Because the checks stop
at the first failure, the issue_underspecified flag is recorded even on rows rejected earlier for
another reason, so we can ask how often it co-occurs with each rejection class. It is raised on 2,027
of the 17,861 judged rows (11.3%), of which 1,235 reach the question as an actionable decision
(1,233 rewritten and 2 rejected for eliding the original) and 792 are rejected before it. Among the
rejection classes, the flag is far more common on over_restrictive rows (14.9%) than on
too_loose (3.8%) or off_target (4.2%) ones—three to four times the rate—which is what
the account above predicts: when a suite silently demands gold’s implementation, the issue usually
fails to mention that requirement too, and the judge observes both halves of the mismatch on the
same row. The reported rewrite rate is therefore conditional by construction: 16.5% of the 7,502
rows whose gold patch and suite are both sound (Table 11), not of everything judged.
E.6

W HAT EXISTING FILTERS CHECK

The claim in Section 4.1 that prior quality filters are one-directional is a statement about their inputs,
not about their care. Each assesses a task by reading the issue, the test patch and the gold patch, and
that evidence can only expose a suite demanding more than the issue states.
SWE- REBENCH fine-tunes a classifier to predict a test patch correctness label, defined as whether
the tests verify the intended fix “without over-reliance on specific implementation details”, and motivates it with the failure of “flawed tests that a correct solution cannot pass” (Badertdinov et al., 2025).
Its V2 successor replaces the classifier with an ensemble of LLM judges whose test-alignment rubric
enumerates seven signals: tests spanning unrelated modules, string assertions on names absent from
the issue, external documentation URLs, vague acceptance criteria, patches touching unrelated files
the tests then require, tests asserting approaches the issue never mentions, and inline tests in the
non-test patch (Badertdinov et al., 2026). Every test-side category is a suite over-constraining the
task; none asks whether a wrong patch would pass. Both calibrate against SWE- BENCH V ERIFIED
human annotations, whose test criterion is likewise that a correct solution not be unfairly rejected
(Jimenez et al., 2023), so the orientation is inherited by construction.
S CALE -SWE goes furthest, and is the sharpest case. LLM judges screen repositories and pull
requests; a unit-test creator agent then synthesises the F2P and P2P suites under an explicit antioverfitting rule forbidding tests to call newly introduced functions directly rather than through the
public API; and a problem-statement agent writes the issue from those tests, so that issue and suite
are aligned by construction (Zhao et al., 2026). Validation is then the standard gate, plus a manual
audit of 100 sampled instances. Every one of these mechanisms points the same way: the antioverfitting rule targets tests demanding too much, and writing the issue from the tests guarantees the

G.1

S TRENGTHENING MECHANICS

Population. We strengthen the 5,351 tasks labelled too_loose in Table 11: 986 from R2EGym, 1,155 from SWE-rebench and 3,210 from Scale-SWE. A task carries this label only after the
judge has determined that its gold patch fully fixes the issue, which is what gives strengthening a
reference fix to check the revised tests against. A further 307 tasks have loose tests and an inadequate
gold patch; these are recorded as gold-patch rejections and excluded from strengthening, because
passing gold is not a useful correctness check when gold itself does not resolve the issue.
What the agent may assert. The strengthening agent is the same model as the judge (Qwen
Team, 2026), given the issue, the full gold patch, the reward contract, shell access to the task’s
container, and editable copies of every file graded by the reward gate. The gold patch is a reference
for correct behaviour but does not define what the tests should require: every added check must
follow from the issue or from established public behaviour of the repository, and the prompt rules out
assertions tied to private helpers, call counts, implementation constants, or gold-specific wording.
This constraint guards against converting a too_loose suite into an over_restrictive one.
The agent records a verdict on whether the suite is weak at all—could a plausible partial fix or
shortcut earn full reward? If it answers not_weak the session ends and the original suite is kept
unchanged (281 tasks, Table 13). Otherwise it strengthens existing reward-gating assertions, adds
parametrized cases, or introduces new test functions, and new node IDs are appended to the reward
contract so that the added tests actually contribute to grading.
Grading. Table 12 lists the source-tree/test-suite pairs. Before the agent starts, the original suite
must yield (gold = 1, buggy = 0); failure excludes the task from this procedure. The agent can
re-check the same condition with its edited tests and extended contract. Submission requires this
separation, which the driver independently verifies after the session. All 4,909 accepted suites passed
the final check.
Isolation and collection. The agent edits copies of the graded tests, not the source code or canonical files. During grading, the copies temporarily replace the canonical tests, which are restored
afterward. After every shell command, the controller compares the repository diff, canonical test
contents, and copied-file inventory with a snapshot. Unauthorized changes are reverted and the
command is marked as failed with an explanation. Added node IDs are checked against an actual
pytest collection before entering the reward gate; unselectable IDs are reported and dropped rather
than silently counted.
Later shortcut evaluation. The controller applies the agent’s proposed source edits and requires
the resulting patch to score 1 under the original suite and 0 under the strengthened suite, recording
the rejecting nodes. Neither score alone demonstrates improvement; the interpretation also depends
on the patch genuinely violating the issue’s requirements. This gate was added after the trained
suites were produced and has been run on 20 tasks, with no measured downstream training effect.

H

ROLLOUT INTEGRITY AND THE EVALUATION SUBSET

An agent with a shell in a repository has two routes to a fixed bug that do not involve solving it: the
repository’s own version-control history, and the network. Section 5.1 summarizes how we close
both; this appendix gives the mechanisms, the evidence that they are needed, and the evaluation
instances that the controls make ungradeable.
H.1

T HE HISTORY CHANNEL

The container images used for training ship a full clone of the repository in which the gold-fix commit is a descendant of the checked-out buggy parent: refs/heads/main typically sits thousands
of commits ahead of HEAD. A single git log -all therefore walks straight to the fix, and does
so entirely offline, so no amount of network isolation closes this route.
The behaviour is learned rather than incidental. On a 9B arm run without the countermeasure, 39%
of rollouts issued a git command reaching past HEAD, and the rate rose from about 5% at step 10
to 99% by step 200, worth +0.30 mean reward (permutation test, p < 10−4 ). It was the dominant
31

reward hack on that taskset, and its growth is the point: under a policy gradient, any channel that
raises reward is reinforced until it saturates.
We prune the history when the container is prepared, in this order:
1. detach HEAD, so that deleting the current branch cannot orphan the checkout;
2. delete every ref via git update-ref -d, which removes packed refs as well as loose
ones;
3. remove every configured remote;
4. expire the entire reflog (git reflog expire -expire=now -all);
5. run git gc -prune=now to drop the now-unreachable objects.
Every step is best-effort, so that a slow garbage collection can never kill a rollout. That tolerance is
precisely why a missing prune is easy to overlook: it fails silently and the run continues. We therefore assert the postcondition rather than any exit status — zero refs and zero remotes remaining —
which is what surfaced the control’s absence on an earlier run after 240 steps. Grading is unaffected
by the prune, because the gold patch is reconstructed from the dataset record rather than from git,
and the pre-agent commit stays reachable through the detached HEAD so that patch capture still diffs
against the right base.
The channel is not uniformly open. SWE-bench Verified ships images whose future history has been
stripped upstream, so that benchmark is clean by construction. SWE-bench Pro is not: its images
retain the gold commit, so the prune is load-bearing there in a way it is not for Verified. Most of our
training corpora likewise ship unpruned clones.
H.2

T HE NETWORK CHANNEL

The network offers both the upstream fix — the merged pull request, the fixed file on a code host,
or a mirror of the benchmark itself — and arbitrary package installation. Sandboxes run under a
CNI egress policy that denies direct outbound traffic and cloud metadata access by default, leaving
reachable only the interception tunnel back to the trainer and an authenticated package proxy scoped
to the sandbox workload.
Proxy configuration is connectivity, not containment. Proxy environment variables are visible to the
model and can be unset by it, so containment has to be enforced at the network layer rather than by
convention. As a check that the policy is actually in force, every sandbox runs a probe at startup
that attempts a TCP connection to the three cloud metadata endpoints (169.254.169.254,
169.254.170.2 and fd00:ec2::254); if any of them answers, the rollout fails outright rather
than proceeding in a misconfigured environment.
H.3

C ORROBORATION

Concurrent work at Cursor identifies the same two channels and adopts the same two mitigations (Jain, 2026). Auditing 731 agent trajectories with a model-based judge that saw the trajectory
but not the outcome, it attributes 57% of reward hacks to locating the merged pull request on the
public web and reproducing it, and 9% to mining the bundled history for the fix commit; 63% of
one frontier model’s successful SWE-bench Pro resolutions retrieved the fix rather than derived it.
Sealing both channels moved that model from 87.1% to 73.0% on SWE-bench Pro and their own
model from 74.7% to 54.0%. Their controls are a repository reinitialized as a single fresh commit,
with the original history restored only at scoring time, and a deny-by-default egress proxy with an
allow-list of package registries.
Two differences are worth naming. First, that study measures a static rate for a fixed model, whereas
the numbers above are measured under a policy gradient, where the rate escalates because the channel is rewarded; an inflated evaluation score can be corrected by tightening the harness, but a policy
that has learned to retrieve has to be retrained. Second, their report notes that SWE-bench addressed
the history channel upstream and that the images they had ingested predated the fix, which is consistent with what we observe: Verified is clean today, Pro is not.

E MPIRICAL AUDIT OF E VALUATION H ACKING IN U NSEALED E NVIRONMENTS

To directly demonstrate how unsealed sandboxes distort evaluation metrics, we evaluate checkpoints
under an unsealed setting where network egress is fully open and the .git repository history remains intact. Table 2 summarizes the trajectory audit across 60 evaluation trials.
When isolation is enforced (27B Baseline), zero trajectories exhibit hacking behavior. However,
when the environment is unsealed, the observed pass rate artificially jumps from 50.0% to 65.0%.
A granular trajectory audit reveals that 19 out of the 39 successful rollouts (48.7%) explicitly exploited sandbox loopholes: 17 rollouts extracted the exact gold fix SHA directly from the git log,
and 7 rollouts fetched external repository source code over the network. Notably, smaller models

I.1

T HE T RAINING DATA

The main runs train on the 7,500-instance curated pool of Section 4.1 and nothing else: 2,086
rows from R2E-Gym, 1,776 from SWE-rebench V2 and 3,638 from Scale-SWE (Jain et al., 2025;
Badertdinov et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), each of which cleared both curation pipelines, with
1,233 carrying an issue rewritten to state what its tests grade. The 4B and 9B runs use this deck
unchanged and differ only in the base model.
The three datasets are served as three environments under a single global deck rather than as three
independently sampled pools. One epoch is therefore every live task exactly once, and the sampler
never draws a duplicate within an epoch; without this, the corpus mixture would drift as the three
pools are consumed at different rates.
A task is retired as mastered when a single group of 8 rollouts solves it unanimously, after which it is
never sampled again. There is no difficulty threshold, no re-admission and no periodic re-check —
the rule is one unanimous group and the task is gone for the rest of the run. This is what Figure 6c
measures, what the eviction-adjusted reward of Appendix J.1 corrects for, and what the probe of
Appendix J tests.
Every instance is prepared with its git history pruned, and every rollout runs under the egress policy
described in Appendix H. Training and evaluation containers are built by the same procedure, so a
shortcut closed for grading is closed for training as well.
The strengthened suites of Section 4.2 are not part of this deck. Strengthening adds 4,889 instances
whose containers must be rebuilt and re-verified before they can be trained on, which lengthens
the run beyond the budget available, so the value of strengthening is established by the controlled
comparison in Section 4.2 rather than carried into the main run.
I.2

T RAINING O BJECTIVE

This appendix states the objective used by every run in the paper. It is a CISPO-style policy-gradient
loss with group-relative advantages, extended with token-level masking of training–inference disagreement. See Appendix A for more details.
Staleness. Rollouts are admitted up to 8 policy versions behind the current weights, which keeps
the generation and training pipelines busy without a synchronous barrier at every step. Key–value
caches are recomputed on weight update rather than carried across versions.
I.3

T HE REWARD AND STOP - CONDITION CONTRACT

A trajectory that reaches the end of its turn budget without fixing the bug and a trajectory that emitted
a malformed tool call on turn three are both failures, but they are not the same failure, and giving
them the same reward teaches the wrong thing. This appendix records how the two are separated.
The base reward is 0/1: a trajectory scores 1 only if the merged fail-to-pass and pass-to-pass identifiers are all present and all passing in the suite’s JUnit XML output, and 0 otherwise. On top of that,
a rollout may terminate early with a stop condition, and two classes of stop condition override the
graded reward to −1. Table 18 gives the mapping.
The division is between trajectories that were never valid attempts and trajectories that were valid but
unsuccessful. A malformed tool call or a response truncated mid-generation produces no evidence
about the model’s ability to fix the bug, and scoring it 0 would place it alongside honest failures and
make it cheap; scoring it −1 makes degenerate output strictly worse than trying and failing. Running
out of turns or context is different: the model made a real attempt and did not finish, which the 0 it
already earns describes correctly, and penalising it further would price exploration.
The asymmetry is load-bearing in one specific way. Under a length penalty, dying early becomes
cheap — the penalty scales with the fraction of the budget consumed, so a rollout that exits after two
turns pays almost nothing and can score better than an honest hundred-turn failure. The −1 override
for truncation exists to close that gradient. The main run carries no length penalty, which makes

D ESIGN

Two hypotheses are worth separating, and they make opposite predictions.
Retirement on luck. A task whose true success probability is p is retired with probability p8 on
any appearance — 17% per draw at p = 0.8 and 43% at p = 0.9. Under this hypothesis
a substantial share of the deck left on a lucky streak rather than on mastery, and re-scoring
the retired population at any later step should find it well below the 1.0 it scored on the way
out.
37

Forgetting. Retirement was sound, but competence decays afterwards because no gradient preserves it. Under this hypothesis the retired population scores near 1.0 shortly after retirement and degrades as training continues.
We therefore re-score the same retired tasks at two checkpoints. From each run’s step-350 eviction
state we sample 300 retired tasks from each of the three corpora and score them at 4 rollouts per
task. The decks are then held fixed and re-scored at step 500, so the step-350-to-500 comparison is
within-task: identical instances, identical grading, only the weights differ. Scores are reported under
the same reward contract used in training, including the −1 overrides of Appendix I.3.
Cutting each arm’s decks from its own eviction state matters for how the numbers are read. It makes
1.0 a value that run itself measured on exactly those rows, rather than a threshold inherited from the
other arm. It also means the reference point is equally valid for both, which a shared deck could not
provide.
The probe runs through the evaluation path with no trainer present, so the weights are structurally
incapable of moving and none of the orchestrator’s batch machinery — buffering, online difficulty
filtering, the zero-advantage filter, off-policy caps — is in play. The grading contract, sampling
parameters, sandbox configuration and concurrency ceiling are lifted from each run’s own training
configuration rather than from the shipped evaluation template, since the template enables three stop
conditions that both runs disable and would otherwise measure grader mismatch as forgetting.
That the harness discriminates at all is confirmed by scoring tasks still live in the 4B’s deck at step
350: those return 0.426 on R2E-Gym and 0.299 on SWE-rebench, against 0.952 and 0.946 for the
retired decks at the same checkpoint. The evaluation is not returning high scores indiscriminately.
The live pools are an anchor rather than a matched control — eviction removes easy tasks by definition, so the live pool is enriched for hard ones by construction, and the gap between the two is not
an effect size.

on R2E-Gym and 0.054 on SWE-rebench. Retirement on luck predicts far more than this: a task
retired on a lucky streak has a true success probability around the 0.8–0.9 that makes p8 appreciable
in the first place, so the retired population would return a mean near that value, not within five points
of 1.0. Retirement on luck is therefore not what the rule is doing at this scale: at step 350 those tasks
really were mastered, and the p8 concern, though sound in principle, does not describe this run.
Note that the probe draws 4 rollouts where the rule fired on 8, so the “solved 4/4” column estimates
E[p4 ] while the rule is E[p8 ]. Since p8 ≤ p4 , that column overstates how re-evictable the deck is,
which is the conservative direction for this conclusion. Mean reward estimates E[p] and is unbiased
by group size, so it is the column to read.
This moves the question rather than settling it. If the deck was correctly identified as mastered,
the decline in held-out score after step 250 cannot be explained by a deck silently made harder by
chance.

R ELATED WORK

Training SWE agents. SWE-Gym and R2E-Gym provide executable repository environments
for training agents and verifiers (Pan et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2025). SWE-smith provides task and
training resources, while DeepSWE releases Qwen3-32B RL training on R2E-Gym (Yang et al.,
2025a; Agentica & Together AI, 2025). AgentFly supplies a Qwen3-32B SWE example with containerized tools and reward execution (AgentFly Contributors, 2026). Prime-RL publishes GLMAir and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking bash-harness examples with different optimizers and training
scales (PrimeIntellect, 2026b;a). NeMo’s Qwen3 recipe precedes asynchronous SWE2 training
with a single-step tool-use stage, using OpenHands-based environments and Apptainer sandboxes
(NVIDIA NeMo, 2026). These differences in initialization, interface and update rules limit what
an adaptation can establish about its source recipe. Our focus is on diagnosing whether an asynchronous recipe can acquire and sustain success on a fixed, solvable task pool before applying it to
broader training data; Appendix B provides detailed recipe comparisons.
Same-base-model training. Dressage reports Qwen3.5-4B learning through Claude Code, with
synchronous GRPO, integrity-gated rewards, importance weighting and reference KL; its SWEGym report shows 80 updates and a SWE-bench Verified increase from 32.6% to 37.8% (Dressage
Contributors, 2026c). In concurrent work, FrogNano reports 61.5% using Leaf and DPPO, with
task regeneration, optimizer resets and length shaping (Kim et al., 2026). Both establish successful
learning in their reported conditions. FrogNano’s regenerated tasks and optimizer resets address
curriculum development, while our fixed-pool study tests whether learned performance survives
continued optimization without those resets.
Asynchronous RL and execution consistency. AReaL decouples generation from optimization,
controls rollout staleness, and recomputes cached attention states after in-flight weight updates (Fu
et al., 2025). Yao et al. (2025) show that distinct rollout and training implementations can assign different token probabilities even with identical weights, introducing an additional source of off-policy
mismatch. CISPO and GSPO modify importance weighting, while other work bounds policy age or
changes which updates are admitted (MiniMax, 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2026). Dressage studies policy-version bounds for partial rollouts and separately reports synchronous enginebalancing performance (Dressage Contributors, 2026b;a). Nebius reports degradation after a serving upgrade changed sampling filters and recovery when settings were restored (Golubev et al.,
2025). Our evidence connects serving admission, observed policy age and training mismatch in
asynchronous SWE rollouts, with a separate fixed-weight execution audit. It does not identify each
of these mechanisms from one aggregate divergence metric.
MiMo-V2.6 jointly scales RL compute, environment diversity and grader compute (Xiaomi MiMo
Team, 2026). Its report was uploaded on September 21, 2026, while our study was in progress. That
report describes asynchronous partial rollouts with a reported staleness bound of four, KV rebuilding
after policy updates, and zero-advantage filtering (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Sections 4.1 and 5.1).
Its Muown optimizer uses β1 = β2 = 0.95 in the Adam component and carries FP32 master
weights and Muown row state from SFT. Those choices overlap our recipe concerns but do not
constitute the same optimizer or initialization. Retaining the original behavior log-probabilities
for importance weighting is compatible with rebuilding KV for subsequent generation; these are
different operations.
MiMo’s Sample Mixer accounts for rollout duration and group acceptance rates, while its predictive
dispatch limits queueing that can increase policy age (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 6.3). The
40

scheduling simulation excludes staleness expiry, so it is not a matched-cap measurement of realized policy age. Its consistency mechanisms include quantized weight alignment, routing replay
and sampling-candidate-set replay (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 6.4). These concurrent results complement our fixed-weight numerical audit and matched-cap admission measurements with
evidence from a different model scale and workload.
Continuation and credit assignment. SWE-Master reports collapse under a DeepSWE-style
truncation-masking treatment and stable learning with forced submission and stop-dependent reward modulation (Song et al., 2026). Nebius warns that masking looping trajectories can remove
useful negative feedback (Golubev et al., 2025); SkyRL-Agent reports successful training with recovery hints and truncation loss masks (Cao et al., 2025). NeMo configures negative message-token
advantages for invalid calls or malformed thinking (NVIDIA NeMo, 2026). These precedents make
the full handling and training-eligibility contract the relevant unit of comparison. Removing recovery or enforcing our particular format is not a universal prescription.
MiMo-V2.6 reviews specification–test alignment, checks reward stability across repeated executions, and audits false-positive and false-negative feedback (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 4.2.1). Environment cleanup, network isolation and trajectory audits further protect reward
integrity (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 4.2.6). Its groupwise grading distinguishes passing
solutions by quality, and segment-level penalties reshape credit for faulty intermediate actions (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 4.3). Offline rubric synthesis can create reward differences even in
an all-test-passing group; online advantage redistribution operates on mixed-outcome groups. These
methods enrich the signal before filtering and do not contradict excluding groups whose final advantages are all zero. Its group-relative length penalty and advantage rebalancing also illustrate why a
shaping term must be assessed within its complete recipe.
Repeated exposure and data utilization. Long-run stability is not unique to this work: SAO
reports roughly a thousand training steps and CANOPY reports 260 steps without degradation (Hou
et al., 2026; Pu et al., 2026). Update counts alone do not give comparable exposure because group
sizes, task pools, optimizer resets and filtering differ.
The MiMo flagship curves cover 30 large updates with 1,568 prompts and sixteen rollouts per
prompt; a separate grading comparison continues through 52 updates (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026).
The report also includes successful RL on a distilled Qwen3.5-9B initialization and evaluation across
held-out harnesses (Xiaomi MiMo Team, 2026, Section 7). That study measures broad capability
improvement and harness transfer. Our fixed-pool experiments deliberately probe continued optimization after training tasks have been learned, exposing deterioration that changing task mixtures
can conceal.
Zero-advantage filtering is also established (Yu et al., 2025b; Mistral AI, 2025). We examine nonzero
advantages and retirement on broader data. FrogNano’s solve-probability-calibrated task synthesis is
a complementary way to seek informative groups; neither approach guarantees that a larger effective
batch improves held-out performance.
RL task quality. UTBoost, SWE-ABS, and Probe to Generate strengthen repository-level repair
benchmarks by exposing weaknesses in their test suites (Yu et al., 2025a; 2026; Li et al., 2026);
Rajan (2026) also audits reward hackability in code RL training environments. Improving tests
for training is not new: ACECODER synthesizes tests for code-model RL, HardTests studies the
downstream effects of test quality, and EvolveCoder iteratively refines tests using candidate-solution
execution feedback (Zeng et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Ruan et al., 2026). We study this problem in
repository-level, multi-turn SWE training, where the issue, tests, and gold patch jointly determine
the reward’s validity. Our paired strengthening experiment holds task identities fixed and changes
the test suite used to compute training reward, measuring how this intervention affects held-out agent
performance rather than only the scores assigned to a fixed set of candidate patches.
Environment integrity and measurement. Prior audits identify repository-state leakage in SWE
environments (Kahn et al., 2025). Hardened tests and integrity gates answer different questions
from compliance with a harness-specific interaction policy. Seed and framework sensitivity also
motivate consistent evaluation and explicit uncertainty (Hochlehnert et al., 2025; Miller, 2024). We
