Enabling Token-wise Importance Sampling in Off-Policy RLVR
with Proximal KL Regularization
Hao Bai

Jiarui Yao

Tong Zhang

arXiv:2610.18738v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Abstract
In reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), asynchronous generation, differences between rollout and training policies, and response reuse across optimizer minibatch
updates can all produce off-policy data. Token-wise importance weighting allows policy-gradient
methods to reuse these responses without multiplying likelihood ratios across a long response,
but it generally biases the gradient. We prove that, under coverage and local expressiveness conditions, adding full future KL credit makes the exact proximal optimizer the update’s unique
stationary policy. The result holds even when responses come from a mixture of older policies and requires neither full-response importance weights nor a fitted critic. We also prove
convergence of continuous-time tabular population updates under persistent coverage and timevarying behavior policies, and give a finite-sample reward guarantee for linear logistic models
under additional assumptions. These results motivate Proximal KL, a practical off-policy RLVR
method that separates the proximal reference from the sampling policy and replaces ordinary
PPO clipping with bounded future KL credit. Across all seven matched off-policy comparisons
on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, Proximal KL achieves higher micro-averaged accuracy than
GRPO over eight math benchmarks, spanning two learning rates, response reuse, and rollout
staleness. The absolute improvement in micro-averaged accuracy reaches 2.58%. A separate
on-policy comparison shows comparable aggregate accuracy for the two methods. These gains
accompany stable optimization: in the periodic-refresh experiments, Proximal KL avoids the
training collapses observed in PG and CISPO, attains higher training accuracy than GRPO,
and reaches higher accuracy in fewer policy updates. To explain these gains, ablations first
compare how off-policy sampling is corrected: token-wise weighting outperforms sequence-level
and unweighted controls. With token-wise correction retained, future KL feedback reduces sensitivity to the tested baseline perturbation relative to PPO clipping. The extent of this feedback
matters as well: a finite future cost horizon improves AIME24 mean@16 and pass@16 over the
full tail, while fixed-policy measurements show that truncation reduces correction variance over
the tested horizons.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) optimizes a policy from verifier-scored responses, often generated by a behavior policy that differs from the policy being optimized. This
off-policy mismatch has several sources. Asynchronous systems overlap generation and optimization, so responses may come from earlier policy versions (Fu et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026; Zhu
et al., 2025; RadixArk, 2026). Reusing collected trajectories across optimizer minibatch updates
introduces mismatch as the current policy changes, even when collection is synchronous. We study
the common problem of learning from these off-policy responses.
1

Policy-gradient methods such as GRPO and DAPO use token-wise importance ratios (Shao et al.,
2024; Yu et al., 2025). Exact importance correction of a response-level objective instead multiplies
the ratios across the response, which can have high variance for long responses. Token-wise weighting
avoids this product but generally biases the gradient. We ask when an update based on token-wise
weights and sampled rewards, without a fitted value or Q function, still retains the proximal target.
This target is the optimizer of a KL-regularized objective.
We study a proximal objective that penalizes the reverse KL divergence from the current policy to
a reference policy held fixed during each optimization round (Geist et al., 2019; Tomar et al., 2022).
The reference defines the improvement target, while the behavior policy determines the sampling
distribution. Following batch-size-invariant policy optimization (Hilton et al., 2022), we separate
these policies so the target remains fixed even when the data-generating distribution differs from
the current policy. For autoregressive responses, the regularized policy gradient assigns each token
the current and all future token log-ratio costs, as expressed by Future-KL credit (Yao et al., 2026).
We combine this full credit with a single importance ratio for the current token.
Our main result shows that, under coverage and local expressiveness, the expected token-wise update
with full future KL credit vanishes if and only if the learner equals the proximal optimizer. The
result holds for any supported stale behavior policy or mixture of behavior-policy versions, although
the expected update can differ from the exact proximal gradient away from the optimizer. The proof
uses the soft path-consistency identity established by PCL and Trust-PCL (Nachum et al., 2017,
2018). With independent tabular logits and persistent coverage, we further prove convergence of
continuous-time updates (Appendix E).
To study learning from sampled responses, we also give a finite-sample reward guarantee after repeated reference refreshes for a linear logistic model, whose token logits depend linearly on trainable
parameters through fixed features (summarized in Theorem 2, with the precise statement in Appendix F). Under its assumptions, the final policy approaches the comparator’s expected reward
for a single response. Its mismatch condition bounds the sum of learner-weighted squared token log
ratios along each response path, and the update never forms the response-level product of ratios.
This analysis motivates a practical algorithm that can also be applied to nonlinear models such as
Transformers.
Guided by these results, we develop Proximal KL Regularization (Proximal KL). The practical
update records each token’s behavior probability, periodically refreshes the proximal reference, and
subtracts bounded future KL costs from a group-relative reward advantage before applying the
token-wise importance weight. A bound on each log-ratio cost limits individual contributions, while
a tunable horizon limits how many future costs are included. The update omits ordinary PPO
clipping. Section 4 discusses the trade-off between bias and variance when bounding the costs, and
Section 6.4 evaluates these choices empirically.
Contributions. To our knowledge, this is the first stationary-policy guarantee for critic-free tokenwise importance weighting with full future KL credit under supported stale behavior mixtures. We
also prove tabular convergence and, for a realizable linear logistic model, a finite-sample reward
guarantee under correct-solution coverage using a full-credit group update with repeated reference
refreshes (Theorem 2). Our empirical comparisons show that Proximal KL achieves higher microaveraged held-out accuracy than GRPO in all seven matched off-policy settings in Section 6.3.
These gains span eight math benchmarks, 1.7B and 4B models, and the tested learning rates and
rollout staleness levels, including fresh rollouts reused across optimizer minibatches. In the separate
on-policy comparison, Proximal KL attains aggregate accuracy comparable to GRPO. Complementary periodic-refresh experiments show that Proximal KL sustains training gains through the
collapses observed in PG and CISPO and attains higher training accuracy than GRPO at compara2

ble optimizer-update counts (Section 6.2). The ablations connect these outcomes to update design:
current-token importance correction outperforms Sequence-level IS and IS-free settings, and future
KL feedback is less sensitive to the tested baseline perturbation than PPO clipping. The tightest
tested future-cost bound combines strong accuracy with smaller gradient-norm excursions, while a
finite horizon outperforms both strong local and full-tail corrections. Fixed-policy measurements
confirm that truncation reduces correction variance over the tested horizons (Section 6.4).

Related Work

Proximal updates. Proximal methods control policy changes relative to a reference policy, as in
TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015). Regularized MDPs and mirror-descent policy optimization formalize
KL-based updates (Geist et al., 2019; Tomar et al., 2022). Batch-size-invariant policy optimization
(Hilton et al., 2022) distinguishes the proximal reference from the behavior policy, separating the
optimization target from the sampling distribution. AReaL and AsyncWebRL apply this distinction to asynchronous optimization (Fu et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026). Slime and Miles support
asynchronous rollout and training pipelines that reduce bubble time (Zhu et al., 2025; RadixArk,
2026). FRPO derives the on-policy Future-KL gradient identity for autoregressive regularization
(Yao et al., 2026). We use this identity for expected reward regularized against a fixed proximal
reference (Appendix D.2), then characterize the stationary policy when full future credit is combined with a single current-token importance ratio under behavior-policy mismatch, including stale
behavior mixtures.
PCL establishes soft path consistency and minimizes the corresponding errors on off-policy trajectories (Nachum et al., 2017). Trust-PCL extends this approach to relative-entropy regularization
against an updated reference (Nachum et al., 2018). Soft Q-learning learns soft Bellman values
and an associated sampling policy (Haarnoja et al., 2017); Unified PCL represents policy and value
with one Q model (Nachum et al., 2017). Schulman et al. (2017a) derive regularized Monte Carlo
policy gradients and relate them to soft Q-learning with value fitting. Our analysis concerns the
stationary policy of an update that combines policy scores with token-wise importance weights,
sampled rewards, and full future credit. The update requires no fitted values or optimization of a
consistency residual (Section 5).
Policy-gradient methods. REINFORCE uses sampled policy scores (Williams, 1992), while
PPO uses a clipped policy-gradient surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017b). For language-model reasoning, GRPO constructs group-relative advantages (Shao et al., 2024), and DAPO develops clipping
and sampling choices (Yu et al., 2025). V-trace and Retrace correct bootstrapped value or Q targets
for off-policy data using truncated importance traces (Espeholt et al., 2018; Munos et al., 2016). Vtrace also weights its policy update by a current-action ratio. Their fixed-point guarantees concern
corrected value learning. We establish the unique stationary policy of the actor update itself, using
sampled rewards, full future KL credit, and current-token importance weighting without a fitted
critic.
Compatible approximation and policy-improvement closure provide structural conditions for policygradient convergence (Agarwal et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023; Bhandari and Russo, 2024). Appendix F relates these conditions to our bounded Gibbs-target representation assumption and its
concrete examples.
We also acknowledge related unpublished work by Audrey Huang and collaborators on coverage
collapse and reference re-anchoring in policy optimization after pre-training (private communication
with Nan Jiang).

2025; Bai et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025; RadixArk, 2026). The behavior policy may therefore differ
from both the current learner and the fixed proximal reference.

Theoretical Guarantees

Full future KL credit gives token-wise importance weighting a common proximal target, even when
the responses come from stale policies. We first state this mechanism for population updates. We
then summarize a finite-sample reward guarantee for a projected group-gradient variant; Appendix F
gives its full specification and proof.
The key observation is that, at the proximal target, the reward minus the full future KL
cost depends only on the current history. The sampled continuation no longer changes this
coefficient. Current-token importance weighting then makes its expected score contribution zero,
even when later tokens were generated by an older policy. The first result shows that, under the
stated conditions, this cancellation also identifies the target uniquely.
Theorem 1 (Token-wise importance weighting preserves the proximal target). Fix πk and assume
the finite-support, behavior-coverage, policy-expressiveness, and population-update conditions (A1)
through (A4) in Appendix E.2. Let Mbehav (θ) be the expected token-wise off-policy policy-gradient
field in Equation (3). For any stale behavior policy, or mixture of behavior-policy versions with their
logged probabilities,
Mbehav (θ) = 0 ⇐⇒ πθ = πk∗ .
Thus the token-wise update has the same unique stationary policy as the proximal objective, although
its expected update can differ from ∇Jk (θ) at other policies.
Here local expressiveness means that parameter changes span every independent token log-odds
direction. The proof and precise assumptions are in Appendix E. Local expressiveness lets us
translate a zero parameter update into a condition at each history, while behavior coverage ensures
that every relevant history contributes to that condition.
Different behavior policies can therefore change the update direction without changing its stationary policy. Convergence additionally requires progress toward that target. For independent tabular
logits and history visit probabilities uniformly bounded away from zero, global trajectories of the
continuous-time population update converge under time-varying behavior policies (Theorem E.2).
This establishes convergence toward a fixed reference’s target when the expected update is available
exactly. To account for sampled responses and repeated reference refreshes, we next study a linear
logistic model with bounded fixed features. A frozen-feature linear output head is one example; applying a linearized model to a nonlinear Transformer additionally requires control of approximation
errors. In this setting, the finite-sample result below shows how repeatedly fitting and refreshing
the reference can improve the final policy’s reward.

Empirical Experiments

We evaluate Proximal KL in two off-policy training settings. Periodic rollout-policy refresh tests
stability across model sizes. Training with controlled rollout staleness combines asynchronous generation with response reuse across multiple optimizer minibatches. These settings test stable optimization and held-out reasoning accuracy when the behavior policy differs from the policy being
optimized.

6.1

Setup

All compared methods omit the KL penalty to the initial model. The reward-advantage normalization follows the training setting below and is shared across methods within each setting.
Working Default. We recommend H = full, β = 0.30, and ϵF = 0.05 as a general starting
configuration for Proximal KL. This choice is shared across all four model sizes in the periodicrefresh experiments. A shorter horizon provides a model-specific adjustment; for 4B, we suggest
H = 32 as a starting point with the same coefficient and cost bound. The ablations examine how
horizon tuning can further improve accuracy. The actual configurations used for the reported results
are listed in Appendices B.2 and C.2.
Training with Periodic Policy Refresh. At each training iteration, we collect a fresh batch
of responses and use that batch once for a single optimizer update. The training-policy weights
therefore change every iteration, whereas the rollout-policy weights are synchronized with the training policy only once every 64 optimizer updates and remain fixed between refreshes. Successive
batches can thus be generated by the same older policy while training progresses. This provides a
controlled simulation of delayed policy synchronization in asynchronous RL, where response generation uses earlier policy versions while optimization continues. We train Qwen3 models (Yang et al.,
2025) on verifiable math tasks using Slime (Zhu et al., 2025), comparing PG, CISPO, GRPO, and
Proximal KL across model sizes under this shared refresh schedule. Reward advantages subtract
the mean reward within each prompt group, without standard-deviation normalization. Figure 3
reports training accuracy, defined in Appendix B.1; all configurations are in Appendix B.2.
Training with Controlled Rollout Staleness. Following AReaL (Fu et al., 2025), we combine
asynchronous rollout generation with multiple optimizer minibatches per batch on filtered math
prompts. We explicitly control the permitted rollout staleness and verify the realized policy-version
gaps from training logs. Reward advantages standardize rewards within each prompt group, as
defined in Equation (A.2). We compare GRPO and Proximal KL, adding PG for the stability test,
and include a separate comparison with fresh rollouts and one optimizer minibatch. PPO clipping is
disabled in that separate comparison because it is designed to discourage large policy changes when
reusing rollout data, whereas this on-policy comparison has zero staleness and uses each batch for
only one update. Held-out evaluation covers MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), OlympiadBench
(He et al., 2024), MinervaMath (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), Gaokao2023MathEn, AIME24/25 (Mathematical Association of America, n.d.), and AMC23/24. MATH500, OlympiadBench, MinervaMath,
and Gaokao2023MathEn use greedy pass@1. AIME24/25 and AMC23/24 use mean@16, the fraction of correct responses among 16 attempts per problem. The learning curves also report pass@16,
the fraction of problems solved by at least one of those attempts. Appendix C.1 gives the reporting
details; Appendix C.2 specifies the data, training configurations, and evaluation budgets.

A finite horizon can outperform the full-tail working default. Starting from the full-tail
working default, we ask whether truncating future credit can further improve accuracy. Figure 9
compares the full tail with intermediate windows and a one-token correction. The comparison
includes both a fixed KL coefficient and a matched product βH, allowing us to examine whether
increasing the local coefficient can compensate for removing future terms.
Both intermediate-window settings outperform the full tail in AIME24 mean@16 and pass@16 near
the end of their shared observation range, including with the default KL coefficient held fixed.
With βH matched, the one-token correction improves mean@16 more slowly than the intermediate
window. Thus, shortening the tail can improve on the working default, while increasing the local
coefficient does not replace future-token credit. This is the practical trade-off suggested by the
theory: truncation omits part of the ideal future credit, but can reduce fluctuations in the correction.
The fixed-policy diagnostic below measures this second effect independently of training.
Truncating the future sum reduces correction variance at fixed policies. To test the
variance argument for horizon truncation in Section 4, we measure how extending the future sum
changes correction variability at fixed policies. For each retained response, we accumulate the scalar
correction at its first token, compute its sample variance across responses to the same prompt, and

17

average these variances across prompts. Policies, responses, the correction coefficient, and the cost
bound are held fixed across horizons. Appendix C.3 specifies the calculation; this paired comparison
isolates the effect of extending the sum on the same data.
Figure 9(c) shows that, with policies and responses held fixed, the average variance of the accumulated correction increases over the tested horizons. Truncating the tail therefore reduces correction
variability in this diagnostic. Longer sums fluctuate more across responses to the same prompt,
on average, even without any change in the policies. This supports horizon truncation as a way
to control fluctuations in future KL feedback. The measurement quantifies variation in the scalar
correction; the training curves show how horizon choice affects held-out accuracy.

Conclusion

Proximal KL combines token-wise importance weighting with future KL credit for off-policy RLVR,
where the behavior policy differs from the policy being optimized. Under the stated coverage
and expressiveness conditions, we prove that the full-tail critic-free update has the exact proximal
optimizer as its unique stationary policy and that tabular population flow converges to this target.
Repeated reference refreshes allow further reward improvement: for linear logistic models, we give
a finite-sample reward guarantee for a projected group update under the stated representation,
sampling, and correct-solution coverage assumptions. The bound depends on an aggregate tokenwise log-ratio second moment. The practical algorithm uses clipped costs and an optional finite
window. The held-out evaluations in Section 6.3 show higher micro-averaged accuracy for Proximal
KL than GRPO in all seven matched off-policy settings, while the separate on-policy comparison
yields comparable aggregate accuracy. Periodic-refresh experiments complement these gains with
stable training where PG and CISPO can collapse, and higher training accuracy than GRPO at
comparable optimizer-update counts. The ablations favor retaining current-token correction and
future credit together, while bounding individual costs and truncating the horizon control correction
fluctuations. An adaptive choice of H could improve this balance by retaining useful future credit
without accumulating unnecessary variability.

This appendix collects the training protocol, model and precision settings, and method-specific
hyperparameters for the periodic-refresh experiments.

B.1

Training Protocol

Each iteration collects a fresh response batch and uses it for one optimizer update. Rollout weights
are synchronized with the training policy every 64 optimizer updates and remain fixed between
refreshes, so successive batches can come from the same older policy while the training policy
changes. The key configurations are given in Appendix B.2. Correct boxed answers receive reward
+1; incorrect or invalid answers receive −1. Training accuracy is the fraction of correct responses,
computed as (1 + R)/2, where R is the mean verifier reward before centering. For the comparison
between Proximal KL and GRPO, we average raw training accuracy over the last 100 updates
observed for both methods within each setting, restricting this summary to settings with at least
100 updates for each method.

This appendix collects the training and evaluation protocols, hyperparameters, and ablation diagnostics for the experiments with controlled staleness. Appendix A describes the practical updates,

C.1

Training and Evaluation Protocols

Rollout generation overlaps with policy optimization, and collected batches are reused across successive optimizer minibatches. We control rollout staleness and verify the realized policy-version
gaps from training logs. The model, data, optimization, reference-policy, and evaluation settings
are given in Appendix C.2.
Figure 5 reports EMA-smoothed online evaluation curves, whereas Table 1 reports separate raw
checkpoint evaluations. Checkpoint scores use neither smoothing nor performance-based checkpoint
selection. The stability test reports held-out accuracy together with policy entropy; the gradient
diagnostics measure norms before optimizer clipping. Appendix C.2 gives the shared settings and
evaluation budgets.
Evaluation metrics. Mean@16 measures correctness per response, while pass@16 measures the
fraction of prompts solved at least once. For independent attempts with per-prompt success prob24

ability p, expected pass@16 is 1 − (1 − p)16 . Because this transformation is nonlinear, the two
metrics describe different aspects of performance. The figures show both; Tables 1 and 2 report
response accuracy only. MATH500, OlympiadBench, MinervaMath, and Gaokao2023MathEn use
greedy pass@1 on 500, 674, 272, and 383 problems. AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and AMC24 use

total number of correct generated responses across the eight benchmarks divided by 4,149 (500,
674, 272, 383, 480, 480, 640, and 720 responses, respectively). Each generated response contributes
once, without counting pass@16 as an additional observation.
