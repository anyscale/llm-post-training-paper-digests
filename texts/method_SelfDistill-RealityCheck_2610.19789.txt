Self-distillation in agentic reinforcement learning has produced a growing number of methods. However,
existing evaluations often reuse published baseline scores under differing experimental conditions,
provide few direct comparisons among contemporaneous methods, and give limited attention to
training stability and computational cost. These limitations obscure the source of reported gains and
the practical strengths and weaknesses of competing methods. Costly agent training further motivates
a common basis for comparison. To address these gaps, we compare ten representative methods against
GRPO on ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, Search-QA, and WebShop across three model sizes. Our study
comprises 132 training runs totaling 12,124.4 recorded H800 GPU-hours, with repeated evaluations
over eight rollout seeds for the pretrained backbones and all eleven trained methods. Beyond final
task performance, we examine training progress, stability, and measured computational cost. Our
evaluation yields three key findings. First, gains over GRPO remain limited in scope, with no method
consistently outperforming it across settings. Second, strong final performance can mask unstable
training. Third, additional training cost does not consistently translate into better performance. We
establish this benchmark to support fair, comprehensive comparison and guide future research toward
reliable and cost-effective improvements in agent training.

Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents interact with environments through sequences of actions and observations (Shridhar et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2022). Reinforcement learning (RL) with sparse outcome rewards
trains these agents from task-level feedback, but provides limited guidance for individual decisions within a
trajectory. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) provides denser supervision for student-generated trajectories
using a teacher branch conditioned on training-only privileged information (Lu et al., 2026; Wang et al.,
2026a). Recent work integrates this teacher guidance into RL through auxiliary losses, advantage modulation,
or reward shaping (Lu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c; Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b), with related
preprints appearing at an average rate of roughly one every two days.1
However, current evaluations of self-distillation in agentic reinforcement learning provide an incomplete basis for
judging progress. Individual gains over established baselines do not establish which contemporaneous methods
are stronger, yet systematic comparisons among these methods remain scarce. Moreover, most comparisons
reuse baseline scores from earlier papers rather than reproducing the baselines under a common protocol,
allowing differences in training and evaluation conditions to be mistaken for methodological progress. Many
studies also emphasize final scores, leaving training instability and the computational cost of improvement less
systematically examined. As Figure 1 illustrates, the proliferation of methods has outpaced the comparable
evidence needed to decide which ones are worth adopting. Closing this gap requires substantial resources:
reproducing agent training is costly, making a shared, systematic comparison difficult to assemble within

To address these limitations, we present an independent benchmark for self-distillation in agentic reinforcement
learning. We compare ten representative methods against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) across four benchmarks—
ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022), Search-QA, and WebShop (Yao et al.,
2022)—and three scales (2B, 4B, and 9B). This comparison spans 12 model–task settings, 132 training runs,
2,772 training-time validation sweeps, and 1,152 evaluation sweeps over eight rollout seeds for pretrained
and trained models. The training runs total 12,124.4 recorded H800 GPU-hours across all four benchmarks.
Shared environments and evaluation procedures provide a common basis for comparison while retaining
method-specific training settings informed by the source papers and implementations. We examine not
only whether gains hold across settings, but also whether they are sustained during training and justify the
additional computation. Our analysis yields three key observations:
(i) Gains over GRPO remain limited in scope, with no method consistently outperforming it across settings.
(ii) Strong final performance does not imply stable training.
(iii) Additional training cost does not consistently translate into better agent performance.

Related Work

Interactive agents and learning signals. Language agents solve tasks through sequential actions and observa-

tions (Shridhar et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2022; Xi et al., 2025). Inference-time methods interleave reasoning and
action (Yao et al., 2023) or refine decisions and outputs through verbal feedback (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan
et al., 2023), without parameter updates. Training methods learn from filtered tool-use annotations (Schick
et al., 2023) and curated interaction trajectories (Qin et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024).
Reward-based optimization learns from sampled behavior (Shao et al., 2024), while online curricula support
continued policy improvement (Qi et al., 2025). Multi-turn RL can nevertheless exhibit unstable updates
and degenerate behavior, motivating careful reward design and rollout sampling (Wang et al., 2025). Our
benchmark focuses on capabilities acquired through parameter updates, rather than improvements obtained
by modifying an agent’s inference procedure.
Distillation and privileged supervision. Distillation transfers teacher predictions, generated sequences, or policy

behavior to a student (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016; Rusu et al., 2016). Training-only privileged
information (Lopez-Paz et al., 2016) and reasoning rationales (Hsieh et al., 2023) enrich supervision beyond
target labels. Privileged supervision therefore seeks to improve the student’s policy without requiring access
to the teacher’s additional information at deployment. Supervising learner-visited states or student-generated
sequences helps reduce distribution mismatch (Ross et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024).

Self-distillation in agentic reinforcement learning. Agentic self-distillation derives teacher supervision from

privileged context available only during training. The evaluated self-distillation methods use four mechanisms.

The Limits of Published Comparisons

Table 1 summarizes how the source papers of the ten compared methods address repeated evaluation, trainingfailure analysis, and measured computational cost. Gaps in this coverage limit assessments of method reliability
and practical value beyond reported performance gains.
Repeated evaluation. Performance differences must be interpreted relative to variation across evaluations. Yet

repeated evaluation is rarely reported in the surveyed sources, making it difficult to distinguish consistent
improvements from favorable sampling outcomes. Reporting variability alongside mean performance is
therefore essential to judging the strength of a claimed advantage.
Training failure analysis. A strong final score does not establish that training is stable. Although some studies

investigate collapse or performance degradation, such analyses are not consistently available across competing
methods. Examining training trajectories is necessary to reveal regressions and failure risks that final scores
can conceal.

Measured cost. Reporting computational cost alongside performance is essential for assessing the efficiency

and practical feasibility of competing methods. Measured cost remains sparsely reported, limiting comparisons
of the resources required to obtain competing methods’ gains. Assessing performance together with training
cost is necessary to determine whether an improvement offers practical value.

None of the surveyed source papers covers all three dimensions. Our benchmark brings them into a common
comparison so that method selection rests on the consistency of measured gains, training stability, and
computational cost rather than a favorable final score alone.

Benchmark Design

Our benchmark examines the value of self-distillation in multi-turn text-agent training, using outcomebased GRPO as the reference. It combines common model–task settings and evaluation procedures with
method-specific objectives and hyperparameters informed by the source papers and released implementations.
Tasks and model backbones. ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) requires agents to complete household tasks

through textual actions and observations. We evaluate 140 games, reporting overall and task-specific success
rates. ScienceWorld (Wang et al., 2022) requires agents to conduct experiments and solve scientific tasks in a
text-based environment. We evaluate 200 tasks across Measure, Test-Cond., Find, Chem-Mix, and Lifespan,
reporting task score. WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) requires agents to find and select products that satisfy a
shopping goal. We evaluate a fixed set of 128 goals, reporting task success and a graded score for partial
goal satisfaction. Search-QA requires retrieval-assisted answers to questions from seven sources. We use a
fixed, seed-0 subset: 500 questions each from NQ, TriviaQA, PopQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and
MuSiQue, plus all 125 Bamboogle questions. We report exact-match accuracy after answer normalization;
Overall gives equal weight to all 3,125 questions rather than to sources or evaluation batches. Combining
these four benchmarks with Qwen3.5-2B, 4B, and 9B yields twelve model–task settings.

Methods. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is the outcome-only reference. GRPO+OPSD and SDAR (Lu et al.,
2026), together with Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026a), ICSD (Lan et al., 2026), and PCSD (Lv et al., 2026),
add auxiliary objectives. RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a), OCSD (Yang et al., 2026c), AgentOPSD (Wang et al.,
2026b), and ADRS (Zhang et al., 2026) use privileged signals to shape credit or rewards; OVCSD (Xia et al.,
2026) adds verified teacher continuations. All methods are evaluated without privileged teacher context. For
Skill-SD and RLSD, we use the skill-bank adaptations; RLSD was originally proposed for verifiable reasoning.
GRPO+OPSD follows SDAR’s constant-gate implementation, whose auxiliary gradient is independent of
teacher scores. Appendices B, C.2, and D detail method adaptations, teacher contexts, and algorithms,
respectively.
Training and evaluation protocol. We train each method in every model–task setting for 100 global steps,

yielding 132 runs, with validation every five steps and method-specific hyperparameters guided by the source
papers and released implementations. Using the same fixed task sets as validation, we evaluate all eleven
trained methods and the pretrained backbones at temperature T = 0.4 over eight rollout seeds, reporting the
mean and sample standard deviation. Final-performance averages assign equal weight to all twelve settings,
using each benchmark’s reported metric; computational-cost comparisons cover the same settings. Across
all four benchmarks, we assess training progress and stability through validation-curve AUC, peak-to-final
declines, and low-performing outcomes, with equal weight on the twelve settings in aggregate comparisons.
Appendices C.1, C.3, and D.1 specify hyperparameters, numerical settings, and the shared training procedure.
Appendices C.4 and C.5 detail the evaluation protocol and experimental records, respectively.

Comparative Performance under Common Evaluation

The central question is whether additional training mechanisms deliver gains beyond a few favorable settings.
We compare each alternative with GRPO across all twelve model–task settings, examining both the frequency
and magnitude of improvements. Tables 2 and 3 report the results for all methods and their pretrained
backbones under the common evaluation protocol. Appendix F.2 analyzes the discrepancy between step-100
validation and repeated final evaluation on Search-QA.
More methods do not imply broad progress. All ten alternatives improve on GRPO in some settings, but

only a minority retain an advantage in aggregate. Only PCSD (54.26, +4.58 points) and AgentOPSD (51.34,
+1.66 points) exceed GRPO’s twelve-setting mean of 49.68; the other eight alternatives fall below it. Since all
ten alternatives yield gains somewhere, isolated positive results do little to establish progress beyond a strong
outcome-only baseline. A broadly applicable training improvement must be assessed across the range of tasks
and model sizes it is expected to support.

Discussion

The faster a field produces new methods, the more it needs a shared basis for judging progress. The
publication pace documented in our survey makes this need particularly pressing for agentic self-distillation.
Without direct, comprehensive comparisons, a growing collection of reported gains need not yield a clearer
understanding of which ideas are worth building on.
This is why we chose to invest in independent evaluation rather than propose another training method. We
aim to clarify where methods improve on strong baselines, where gains break down, and what risks and costs
they entail. A method need not win every setting to be useful, but its value can only be understood when
both its strengths and its limitations are visible.
We hope this benchmark helps researchers choose baselines, investigate unresolved failures, and pursue reliable,
cost-effective improvements. We therefore call for the community to value systematic evaluation alongside
algorithmic innovation. As the literature expands, the community’s capacity for comparison and analysis
must grow with it, so that new methods contribute to cumulative understanding and sustained progress.

9

Conclusion

We benchmark ten representative self-distillation methods against GRPO across four agent benchmarks
and three model sizes. Gains over GRPO remain setting-dependent, with no method uniformly superior;
strong final results can conceal unstable training, and additional computation does not consistently improve
performance. These findings show that progress must be assessed through the breadth of gains, the stability
of learning, and the cost of improvement. By documenting both gains and failures under a common protocol,
our study helps researchers select strong baselines, identify unresolved training challenges, and pursue more
reliable, cost-effective agent training.

Limitations
Limited computational resources and researcher time prevent an exhaustive evaluation of the rapidly growing
literature on agentic self-distillation. We therefore compare ten representative methods with GRPO, covering
different approaches to incorporating teacher guidance. Although this selection does not encompass every
relevant method, the observed setting-dependent gains, training instability, and performance–cost trade-offs
substantiate the need for the comprehensive evaluation advocated in this work. Extending the benchmark to
additional methods would help establish how broadly these patterns hold.

Ethical Considerations
This study compares existing agent-training methods using publicly available models and benchmark datasets.
Experiments are confined to simulated environments and retrieval-based question answering, without humanparticipant studies or deployment on behalf of real users. We identify no significant ethical concerns specific
to this study.

B

Method Descriptions and Adaptations

The eleven methods span outcome-based policy optimization, auxiliary distillation, token- or turn-level
advantage reweighting, reward shaping, and verified teacher continuations. Teachers use the same model
backbone as the student, with privileged information available only during training. Table S1 summarizes
these mechanisms.
We implement the methods in a shared text-agent framework. For Skill-SD and RLSD, we follow the
SDAR repository’s skill-bank adaptations, using the pre-update actor to score student responses under
skill-conditioned prompts (Lu et al., 2026). GRPO+OPSD uses SDAR’s constant-gate auxiliary objective,

13

whose gradient is independent of teacher scores. OVCSD collects additional, environment-verified teacher
continuations.
For AgentOPSD and ADRS, we follow their released implementations of return-based statistics (Wang et al.,
2026b; Zhang et al., 2026). AgentOPSD initializes its belief from the clipped mean of trajectory-deduplicated
training returns. ADRS computes L2 teacher utility over interaction rows grouped by task.
Our ICSD implementation uses an additional gradient-free student-scoring pass to calibrate weights within
each local actor minibatch; this computation is included in the reported training cost. Following Lv et al.
(2026), PCSD uses a frozen, skill-conditioned teacher initialized from the pretrained backbone; we reuse the
fixed reference model’s weights for this branch. The complete objectives and update procedures appear in
Appendix D.

C.1

Training Configuration

We train each of the eleven methods on ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, Search-QA, and WebShop with Qwen3.5-2B,
4B, and 9B, for 132 runs of 100 global steps each. We combine shared optimization defaults with benchmarkand method-specific configurations drawn from the source papers and released code. The sources of these
configurations and the settings for our benchmark extensions are described below; method-specific algorithm
hyperparameters remain fixed across the three model sizes within each benchmark. Tables S2, S3, and S4
summarize common settings, benchmark-specific settings and overrides, and algorithm hyperparameters,
respectively, across all four benchmarks.
Configuration sources. The base learning rate, rollout group size, and default reference KL coefficient follow
Appendix C of SDAR (Lu et al., 2026). For ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-QA, prompt and response
limits, interaction horizons, history windows, PPO minibatch sizes, and invalid-action penalties follow its
released task-specific configurations. ALFWorld and WebShop retain outcome rewards of 10 for success and 0
otherwise; Search-QA uses 1 for a correct answer and 0 otherwise, together with SDAR’s top-three-passage
retrieval configuration. ScienceWorld uses the AgentGym task split (Xi et al., 2025), with 200 evaluation
tasks and 2,059 training instances after removing 61 overlapping task IDs; its episode reward is the simulator’s
final task score divided by 100. These rewards are assigned to interaction rows, with the invalid-action
penalty applied to the corresponding row. Table S3 summarizes these benchmark configurations and their
method-specific overrides.
Baseline adaptations. SDAR and GRPO+OPSD retain SDAR’s reported auxiliary coefficients and gate

settings. For Skill-SD and RLSD, we use the skill-bank adaptations in the SDAR repository: Skill-SD keeps a
constant auxiliary weight of 0.001, while RLSD follows the released Qwen3 scripts’ 100-step linear decay with
initial scale λ0 = 0.5 and multiplier clipping of 0.2.

Method-specific settings from the source papers. AgentOPSD’s algorithm hyperparameters follow Appendix F

of Wang et al. (2026b), including its upper PPO clipping bound of 0.24. OCSD’s selection ratio and reweighting
coefficient follow Table 4 of Yang et al. (2026c); ICSD’s calibration parameters follow Appendix C.1 of Lan
et al. (2026); PCSD’s window and gating parameters follow Table 5 of Lv et al. (2026). OCSD’s Search-QA
warmup ratio of 0.1 and reference KL coefficient of 0.001 follow its released training configuration. The
algorithm-specific values are summarized in Table S4.
Benchmark-specific ADRS settings. We follow the primary GRPO/L2 configurations in Appendix A.5 of

Zhang et al. (2026): ALFWorld uses η = 0.1, batch-wide score normalization, and gate-input normalization;
WebShop uses η = 0.02, per-response score normalization, and no gate-input normalization. The same source
specifies ALFWorld’s entropy coefficient of 0.01 and upper PPO clipping bound of 0.28. For Search-QA, the
teacher-reward scale η = 0.05, batch-wide score normalization, and reference KL coefficient of 0.001 likewise
follow the reported Search-QA configuration. These task-dependent choices preserve the source configurations;
the paper’s normalization ablation supports batch-wide scaling on ALFWorld and Search-QA, and per-response
scaling on WebShop. For the ScienceWorld extension, we adopt the Search-QA teacher-reward scale and
normalization settings, including gate-input normalization, while retaining the common optimization defaults.

D

Training Objectives and Algorithms

Algorithms S1–S12 specify the eleven methods as evaluated in this study. The ten methods beyond GRPO
modify its auxiliary objective, advantage weights, collected trajectories, or reward signal. We define the shared
operations once and expose the method-specific changes below. For more detailed method descriptions and
derivations, we refer readers to the original papers cited in Table S1.
Data and indices. A trajectory is one task attempt, comprising multiple model–environment interactions. The

training batch D contains one row b per active interaction turn; t indexes tokens in that turn’s response. The
binary mask Mbt is one for valid response tokens and zero otherwise. The maps i(b) and g(b) identify the
trajectory and task group of row b; a task group contains all sampled attempts at the same task. Bi and Bg
denote their respective row sets, and g(i) identifies the task group of trajectory i.
Models and token scores. θ0 denotes the initial model parameters, θ the trainable student, and πθ its token

distribution. The old policy θold is refreshed at each training step and held fixed during the ensuing actor
updates; the reference ϕ stays at θ0 throughout training. For each sampled response token, pbt , obt , and fbt
are its log-probabilities under the current student, old policy, and reference, respectively. The teacher score
qbt is the log-probability of that same token given additional training-time context, such as skills or future
observations. These are sampled-token scores, not full-vocabulary distributions, unless stated otherwise.

Operations and gradients. sg(x) keeps the value of x but blocks its gradient. Teacher, old-policy, and

reference scores, rewards, selection decisions, and constructed advantages are detached; gradients flow through
the current student scores p. Products and divisions of token arrays are elementwise, and row quantities
broadcast over tokens. We use the sigmoid σ(x) = (1 + e−x )−1 , log-odds logit(x) = log[x/(1 − x)], and
ReLU(x) = max(x, 0). clip(x, a, b) restricts x to [a, b]; Cϵ (x) = clip(x, ϵ, 1 − ϵ) keeps beliefs away from zero
and one. Other intermediate quantities are local to each method. Subscripted ϵ terms denote numerical
stabilizers, clipping widths, or thresholds, as specified below; hyperparameter values are listed in Appendix C.

Skill-conditioned scoring. SkillReplay(D, ψ) returns teacher scores q: it augments prompts with retrieved

skills, keeps the sampled student responses, and scores them without gradients under model parameters
ψ. Its outputs remain fixed throughout the subsequent actor update. Most skill-replay methods use the
pre-update actor, ψ = θold ; PCSD instead uses the fixed reference, ψ = ϕ. Teacher prompts are left-truncated
as described in Appendix C.

GRPO+OPSD’s constant-gate objective. GRPO+OPSD and SDAR weight their auxiliary loss by λsd and

control the sigmoid gate’s slope with β. In our comparison, GRPO+OPSD (Algorithm S3) sets β = 0,

F.1

Aggregate Progress and Method Rankings

Method rankings depend on whether performance is assessed throughout training or only at its endpoint.
Figure S1 contrasts training-curve AUC with repeated final evaluation and shows the twelve-setting mean
validation trajectories. OCSD leads by AUC, whereas PCSD leads at the endpoint; the latter’s advantage
emerges later in training. The stage-wise comparison makes this change explicit: SDAR leads at steps 20
and 40, OCSD at step 60, and PCSD at steps 80 and 100. An endpoint advantage therefore need not reflect
superior performance sustained throughout optimization. Tables S6 and S7 report the aggregate rankings,
low-performance counts, and stage-wise means for all eleven methods.

F.2

Intermediate Instability and Late Regression

The 4B ALFWorld trajectories illustrate why instability cannot be reduced to a low final score. AgentOPSD
recovers after six consecutive validations at or below 4.3%, whereas GRPO+OPSD loses its early gains
and finishes near zero. OCSD reaches 85.0% before declining to 51.4%, with its final three validations
averaging 50.5%. A criterion based only on near-zero final success would identify GRPO+OPSD but miss
both AgentOPSD’s temporary collapse and OCSD’s sustained late regression. Assessing stability therefore
requires examining the depth and persistence of intermediate declines, even when the final model remains
capable. Figure S2 isolates these histories from Figure 2, retaining GRPO as a common reference.
Decoding-specific collapse. Search-QA reveals that training instability can also depend on the decoding

strategy: a model may retain effective sampled performance despite a collapse under greedy decoding.
Training-time validation uses greedy decoding (T = 0), whereas final evaluation samples at T = 0.4 over
eight rollout seeds; both report exact-match accuracy on the same 3,125 questions. The Search-QA panels in
Figure 2 show these training trajectories; Tables S10 and 3 report their trajectory summaries and sampled
final results, respectively.
For 2B OVCSD, greedy accuracy reaches 38.02% at step 70 and remains at 37.09% at step 80, before falling to
4.77%, 0.22%, 12.86%, and 1.34% at steps 85, 90, 95, and 100. The final-three-validation mean is only 4.81%,
with a 36.67-point peak-to-final drop under the unchanged greedy protocol. Yet the same final checkpoint
scores 33.21 ± 0.33% with sampling, compared with GRPO’s 32.38 ± 0.28%. This is a decoding-specific
collapse: previously acquired greedy performance is lost even though sampling at T = 0.4 still produces
effective task behavior. A sampled endpoint alone would leave this failure mode unobserved.
ICSD shows a milder greedy regression rather than near-zero collapse. Its 2B greedy accuracy peaks at 39.52%
at step 65, falls to 21.73% at step 90, and partially recovers to 31.49% at step 100; the final-three mean is 26.31%.

Per-Run Training Profiles

Aggregate curves can conceal instability confined to a particular task or model size. For every benchmark,
we report each run’s peak, final, and late-training performance alongside its AUC and computational cost.
Peak is the highest validation metric at steps 0, 5, . . . , 100; Step records its first occurrence, Tail-3 averages
steps 90, 95, and 100, and Drop is Peak minus Final in the corresponding metric’s points. AUC integrates
the raw validation curve by the trapezoidal rule and divides by the 100-step horizon; it is not ROC-AUC.
These summaries capture different aspects of the trajectory: a low Tail-3 indicates that weak performance
extends beyond the final measurement, whereas a small Drop does not exclude earlier instability. For example,
AgentOPSD on 4B ALFWorld finishes at its own peak despite the prolonged near-zero phase described above.

H

Literature Survey

H.1

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

Scope. Our survey characterizes recent research on distillation for training multi-turn language-model agents

that interact through tools or environments. It covers text and GUI agents trained with privileged-context
supervision, other on-policy teacher distillation, or offline and iterative trajectory distillation, together with
empirical analyses of these approaches. The common focus is the use of distillation to update the agent’s action
policy. Within this broader landscape, our benchmark compares GRPO with ten representative methods
whose main contributions target specific components of text-agent training, including auxiliary objectives,
credit assignment, reward shaping, and verified teacher continuations (Appendix B).
Search and coverage. We examine first submissions from 25 June to 22 September 2026 (UTC), a 90-day

period, using arXiv metadata retrieved on 23 September. Two title-and-abstract searches pair agent-related
terms with distillation, and self-distillation with reinforcement learning. Deduplication and date filtering yield
635 records, from which the agent–distillation query and interactive-agent keywords identify 84 candidates.
Reviewing these 84 candidates’ titles and abstracts, with full-text inspection where needed, yields 47 papers
that meet the survey scope and form the final inventory. As a coverage check, we verify that it includes every
source paper of the evaluated methods first submitted within this period.

H.2

Publication Statistics

Counting each unique arXiv paper once by its first-submission date gives 47 papers over 90 days, or
approximately one paper every two days (0.5222 per day). The inventory includes 32 papers on privilegedcontext policy supervision (P), 10 on other on-policy distillation and related empirical frameworks (O), and 5
on trajectory-based policy distillation (T). The full inventory, with titles, first-submission dates, supervision
categories, and arXiv links, appears in Tables S17–S20.
