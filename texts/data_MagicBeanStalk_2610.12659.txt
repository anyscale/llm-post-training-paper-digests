A BSTRACT
Data-centric scaling of RLVR has largely focused on collecting or generating verifiable training problems. However, corpus size alone does not determine whether
the dataset provides a useful learning signal for a target policy. To guide the synthesis of effective RLVR data, we analyze the RLVR objective and experimentally
characterize dataset effectiveness as a function of its problems, the target policy,
and the training rollout budget. Under this characterization, we show that a large
open-source math corpus pooled from widely used RLVR datasets falls almost
entirely into a zero-advantage trap: for a fixed target policy and rollout budget,
roughly 88% of its problems are solved in every rollout or failed in every rollout. Filtering concentrates what remains, but it can only select, discarding the
hard problems. Effective synthetic problems should therefore require the reasoning operations and compositions required to solve those hard problems. Yet their
difficulty must be calibrated, so that some target-policy rollouts succeed, some
fail, and at least one complete correct solution fits within the training budget.
Prompting a teacher to vary hard problems directly does not achieve this. Guided
by these empirical design principles, we introduce MagicBeanStalk, a top-down
decomposition and bottom-up recursive data-synthesis algorithm that grows seed
problems into families of substantively new, policy- and budget-calibrated training problems while preserving the seeds’ reasoning blueprints. Across 8 math
reasoning benchmarks, continual RLVR on MagicBeanStalk problems improves
gpt-oss-120b from 61.2 to 69.8 (+8.6) and DeepSeek v4 Flash from 72.4 to 77.9
(+5.4) on a 16K-token budget. Training on the full 78K source corpus improves
gpt-oss-120b by only +1.1 and its best filtered subset by +5.8. In finance, we synthesize over 14K verifiable training problems from only 340 solutions and improve
Qwen3 by +6.9 and +2.4 points on SecQue and FinanceBench, respectively.

I NTRODUCTION

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become key for improving reasoning
models, but as policies become stronger, scaling RLVR increasingly becomes a data problem requiring a continual supply of verifiable data. Existing efforts have expanded this supply by collecting human-authored problems for math, coding and other domains (Chen et al., 2025; Luo et al.,
2025; Albalak et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025) , as well as by developing procedural environments that can generate multiple problems per environment (Stojanovski et al., 2025;
Lacombe et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025) . However, sourcing high-quality human problems become
increasingly hard as we run out of internet data. And procedural environments are typically handengineered, limited to a set of domains, and often produce problems that vary surface details within
a fixed reasoning structure. As a result, recent approaches synthesize surrogate tasks from Internet data (Lu et al., 2026) , yet more data does not necessarily ensure the problems provide useful
learning signal for a target policy and training budget.
In this work, we return to the binary-reward RLVR objective and characterize dataset effectiveness
as a function of three interacting components: the problems it contains, the target policy, and the

key principles to guide the synthesis of effective RLVR data. We demonstrate that more data does
not necessarily lead to better downstream performance, and a large corpus can provide little learning
signal when most problems fall into what we call the zero-advantage trap (i.e. problems are either
too easy or too hard for the target policy under the training budget). Specifically, in a corpus of approximately 78K open-source math problems, roughly 88% fall into this trap for gpt-oss-120b
at a 32K rollout budget. Filtering alone is limited to selection, and disregards the hard problems that
sits at the frontier of the target policy and expose the reasoning capabilities the policy lacks.
Our key insight is to repurpose the hard problems as seeds from which to grow substantively new
problems whose solutions require the same reasoning operations, but whose difficulty is calibrated to
produce mixed rollout outcomes and admit at least one complete correct solution within the training
budget. However, the obvious baseline—directly prompting a strong teacher to produce variations of
the hard seed— performs no better than training on the best problems already present in the corpus.
This motivates a more structured construction, MagicBeanStalk (MBS). MBS is a top-down decomposition and bottom-up recursive synthesis method that grows a small collection of seeds into
families of policy- and budget-calibrated RLVR problems. MBS decomposes each seed into a validated tree of simpler subproblems, synthesizes substantively new leaf problems, and recombines
them while preserving the seed’s reasoning blueprint (Figure 1). MBS escapes the trap where direct synthesis does not: from hard seeds on which gpt-oss-120b scores 0/8, it produces inbudget Goldilocks problems for 74.7% of seeds versus 43.8% for direct synthesis. In a matched
training control on a shared seed population, MBS reaches 69.2, versus 63.9 for direct synthesis
and 65.3 for training on the seeds themselves. On gpt-oss-120b, continual RLVR raises the
avg across 8 math reasoning benchmarks (AIME 24/25, AMC, HMMT-F/N, Minerva, Olympiad,
Omega-500) from 61.2 to 69.8 (+8.6 points) at a 16K-token budget. The same data applied to
DeepSeek v4 Flash raises its average from 72.4 to 77.9 (+5.4 points) under the same budget.
Further, MBS gains persist at 24K and 32K and on MathArenaApex (Dekoninck et al., 2026) they
exceed the best baseline’s gain at every budget. Beyond math, we also apply MBS to finance, a
specialized domain where annotation is expensive and verifiable RL data is scarce. Starting from
only 340 expert-curated seed questions, MBS synthesizes over 14,000 grounded financial reasoning
problems. Continual RLVR on this data improves Qwen3-4B by +6.9 pass@1 on SecQue and +2.4
on FinanceBench.

We characterize the learning signal available in a large corpus of open-source mathematical reasoning problems using the success rate. The corpus combines problem from DAPO-Math17K (Yu et al., 2025b), Skywork-OR1-RL-Data (He et al., 2025), Turing Open-RL (Patil et al.,
2026), and a subset of StackMathQA Zhang (2024), and contains approximately 78,000 problems. For each problem, we sample N = 8 responses from gpt-oss-120b using rollout budget

RLVR data does not necessarily means better downstream performance. (Appendix B.1 for more)

Therefore, generated problems should be calibrated to the target policy and training rollout budget,
producing both successful and unsuccessful rollouts while admitting at least one complete correct
response within budget. Filtering alone, however, can only select from problems a corpus already
contains. Filtering cannot create new problems targeting capabilities absent from the filtered subset.
Hard problems are specifications for what data to synthesize. Hard problems play a paradoxical
role in RLVR. Problems with zero observed success provide no group-relative advantage in the
sampled group, yet their verified solutions identify reasoning operations and compositions to target
through synthesis. We refer to the essential reasoning operations exposed by a verified solution
and how those operations are composed as reasoning blueprints. We define a verified hard problem
as a solvable problem for which r̄π,L (p) = 0 and the sampled responses terminate normally. The
termination requirement ensures that the observed failures are not artifact of the rollout budget.
To test whether these blueprints provide actionable specifications, we study 98 hard math seeds
and obtain verified traces from a pool of 5 frontier models. For each seed, we generate one simpler
subproblem instantiating some of its blueprint operations and provide its verified solution as a single
in-context example. We verify that it does not reveal the original solution. This intervention enables
the target policy to solve at least one rollout for 78/98 seeds (79.6%) and raises mean accuracy on the
original problems from 0% to 46.9%. Thus, hard problems are not merely training examples. Their
verified solutions provide specifications of what skill a synthetic problem should target. Moreover,
among the 43 seeds with at least two verified blueprints obtained with different teachers, 90.7%
produce the same blueprint or a compatible variant. This further suggests that the blueprint primarily
reflects the problem’s structure rather than a particular teacher style. (See Appendix B.3 for more)
Direct prompting a teacher to generate variations is not enough. Filtering can only select from
problems a corpus already contains while hard seeds specify the reasoning the policy is missing and
left out during filtering. A simple way to synthesize problem starting with hard seeds is to provide the
problem, reasoning trajectory, and its verified solution to a strong teacher . Then perform rejection
sampling on quality control, goldilocks and in-budget solvability. This baseline is straightforward
and we experimentally observed it often retains the mathematical techniques used by the seed. In a
controlled set of experiments, however, we observed seed synthesis provides no clear gain beyond
the seeds. Figure 3(a) shows the training curves. We experimentally found two main reasons for this.

Takeaway. Three principles follow. First, hard problems are specifications. Their verified solutions
name the reasoning operations and skills synthesis should target. Second, synthesis must do what
filtering cannot and should be complementary to dynamic sampling. Synthetic problems should be
substantively new and preserve the hard seed’s reasoning operations and their composition. Third,
usefulness is relative. A problem carries signal for a given target policy and rollout budget, so
generated problems must be calibrated to both. They should yield mixed target-policy rollouts with
a complete correct response within the training budget.

M AGIC B EAN S TALK : E FFECTIVE DATA S YNTHESIS FROM H ARD S EEDS

We now introduce MagicBeanStalk (MBS), a data synthesis algorithm motivated by the findings in
in 2. Each stage in the following subsections is inspired by those principles.
3.1

P RELIMINARIES AND N OTATION

We assume access to a collection of teacher models T := {T1 , T2 , . . . , TM }. In our setting, these
are strong reasoning LLMs used to solve, decompose, synthesize, and verify problems. Additionally,
we define an ordered pool of student models S := {S1 , S2 , . . . , SJ }, ranked from strongest (S1 )
to weakest (SJ ), and a target policy π. In our setting, π is part of the student pool. A problem
is a tuple (p, a∗ ) where p is a question and a∗ ∈ A is its ground-truth answer. In documentgrounded settings (e.g. finance), we let the p = (q, d) where d is a document snippet that contains the
information required to answer q. Decomposition tree. A decomposition tree Gp := ⟨p, τ, a∗ , C⟩
is rooted at (p, a∗ ) with its verified teacher trace τ , and recursively contains a set of K children
C = {Gp1 , . . . , GpK }, each itself a tree on an easier subproblem. A node n with Cn = ∅ is a leaf.

Given a set of verified problems (p, a∗ ), we use a collection of teachers to sample reasoning traces.
Formally, for each problem p, we iterate through teachers T ∈ T in order and, from each, draw up
to Nt reasoning traces and answers (τ (i) , a) ∼ T (p) with i ∈ {1, . . . , Nt }. A trace is verified when
its final answer matches the GT. We accept the first trace satisfying verified(τ, a∗ ) = 1 and store
it as the root’s verified trace in Gp . Here, verified represents a verification function either answer
matching or an LLM-as-a-judge. Problems for which no trace verifies after |T | · Nt attempts are
discarded. A collection of teacher reduces the number of discarded problems due to solvability.
Section B.3 motivates why multiples teacher are beneficial for solution coverage.

Does the subproblem help a student solve the harder problem? At each node, we evaluate the
student pool from weakest to strongest and select the strongest evaluated student with zero empirical

is given the K subproblems and their traces as in-context worked examples and re-tested on the

improvement, we re-prompt the teacher for a fresh decomposition with the previous failed attempts
as negative context, up to Nd retries. Nodes whose decompositions remain ineffective are dropped.
In our experiments N = 8 and Nd = 3, and we recurse up to depth D = 3. The effectivity test is
motivated by the second design principle (See 2 and B.3).

E XPERIMENTAL R ESULTS

In this section, we first quantify qualified yield, blueprint preservation, data quality, and downstream
performance relative to the source seeds and seed synthesis in a control experiment. We then evaluate
continual RLVR across 8 math benchmarks, transfer to a different target policy, performance beyond
the training response budget, and document-grounded finance. Unless specifically mentioned, for
most experiments involving training, we use as 16K rollout budget due to computational constrains.
4.1

F ROM Z ERO -A DVANTAGE S EEDS TO E FFECTIVE RLVR DATA

Qualified synthesis yield from zero-signal seeds. We randomly sample approximately 1,250
source seeds on which gpt-oss-120b obtained 0/8 correct rollouts at 16K. From the same seeds,
MBS produces 3,370 descendants and seed synthesis produces 2,494. A descendant is qualified if it
lies in the Goldilocks band and has at least one correct rollout that terminates normally within 16K.
MBS yields qualified descendants from 74.7% of the seeds, compared with 43.8% under seed synthesis; its candidate-level qualified rate is also higher (42.8% versus 31.7%; Table 8). Our algorithm
notably converts more zero-signal hard seeds into usable training data.
Blueprint preservation and novelty. For each method, GPT-5.6 scores 300 math and 100 finance
seed–candidate pairs from 1 to 5 for blueprint preservation and substantive novelty. In math, our
method preserves the blueprint comparably to seed synthesis while producing more novel problems.
Concretely, 19.7% of MBS pairs score at least 4 on both criteria, compared with 3.0% for seed
synthesis. In finance, the corresponding rates are 86.0% and 17.0% (Figure 4, left).
Data quality analysis. Three frontier models (GPT-5.6, Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.1 Pro) assess
whether each of 150 math and 50 finance randomly sampled problems are well-posed and its GT answer is correct. For finance,we also include the source document. All three judges reject only 8.0%
of the math answers and none of the finance answers (Table 4). We report all agreement metrics but
emphasize the usage of unanimous rejection. Failure to verify an answer may also reflect the judge’s

R ELATED WORK

Scaling Training Data for RLVR. Collected corpora supply verified math and code problems (Albalak et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025;
Lu et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026; Lu et al.).
Others generate tasks in hand-designed environments (Lacombe et al., 2025; Stojanovski et al.,
2025), adapt difficulty to the policy (Zeng et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026), or mine tasks from Internet text (Lu et al., 2026). Closest to us, a teacher writes new problems from seeds or con-

9

cepts for SFT (Toshniwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025), VLMs (Liao et al., 2025; Acuna et al.,
2025b; Jung et al., 2026; Acuna et al., 2025a), and RLVR (Yu et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2025;
2026). Rather than collecting problems, hand-engineering environments, scaling quantity, or asking
a teacher to synthesize based on seeds, we analyze what makes a synthetic problem useful for RLVR,
then design a top-down, bottom-up recursive algorithm that turns hard seeds into new, policy- and
budget-calibrated problems.
Algorithmic RL for Hard Problems. Dynamic sampling discards zero-advantage groups (Yu et al.,
2025b), BroRL scales rollouts per prompt (Hu et al., 2025). Recent work guide exploration with
oracle prefixes, hints, or off-policy traces (Qu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These
methods modify the training algorithm or optimizer. Our work is complementary and focuses on the
data rather than the modifying the algorithm with the same problems.

C ONCLUSION

We show the value of RLVR data is relative to the problem, target policy, and rollout budget. We also
show hard problems should guide the type of problems to synthesize. Based on these findings, we
design key principles to guide effective data synthesis algorithms. MBS instantiates these principles
by decomposing hard seeds into validated reasoning trees and regrowing them into substantively
new, policy- and budget-calibrated problems. Our data improves gpt-oss-120b by +8.6 and
DeepSeek v4 Flash by +5.4 points across 8 math benchmarks. In finance, 340 expert questions
yield over 14,000 verifiable training problems and improve Qwen3 by +6.9 and +2.4 points on
SecQue and FinanceBench, respectively.

W HAT M AKES A P ROBLEM U SEFUL FOR RLVR?

B.1

A LARGE CORPUS CAN PROVIDE LITTLE LEARNING SIGNAL

We next use the empirical success rate to characterize the learning signal available in a large
corpus of open-source mathematical reasoning problems. The corpus combines problems drawn
from DAPO-Math-17K (Yu et al., 2025b), Skywork-OR1-RL-Data (He et al., 2025), Turing OpenRL (Patil et al., 2026), and a subset of StackMathQA Zhang (2024), and contains approximately
78,000 problems. For each problem, we sample N = 8 responses from gpt-oss-120b using
rollout budget L. Despite the size of the corpus, only 12.2% of its problems fall within the observed Goldilocks band (L = 32K). As shown in Figure 2, most problems are solved in all rollouts
and therefore have empirical success rate r̄π,L (p) = 1 while a 6.3% are not solved in any rollout. Therefore, roughly 88% of the corpus falls in the zero-advantage trap. They contribute no
group-relative advantage.
We then test whether the empirical Goldilocks criterion identifies problems that are more useful
for downstream RLVR. Starting from the same initial gpt-oss-120b policy, we compare three
computationally tractable training sets. We first construct a ∼7K-problem proxy whose proportions of hard, Goldilocks, and stale problems match those of the full open-source corpus. From
this proxy, we extract the ∼862 Goldilocks problems and a stricter 696-problem subset that also
demonstrates in-budget solvability under the 16K training rollout limit. All conditions use the same
training recipe and are evaluated after approximately one epoch. As shown in Figure 3(b), both
the Goldilocks-only and the Goldilocks plus in-budget subsets reach a higher average accuracy than
the full-mixture proxy, while training on an order of magnitude fewer problems. Thus, Goldilocks
filtering concentrates useful RLVR signal. Filtering, however, can only select. It retains the small
fraction of a corpus that already yields mixed outcomes and discards the rest, including the hard
problems on which the policy never succeeds. Most importantly, filtering cannot produce a problem
for a capability the corpus does not already cover.
B.2

D IFFICULTY IS RELATIVE TO THE POLICY AND TRAINING BUDGET

Problem difficulty in RLVR depends on both the target policy and the rollout budget used during training. We demonstrate each dependence through a controlled experiment. Let fi ∈
{stop, length} indicate whether response terminates normally or reaches the rollout limit L. We
say that a problem has demonstrated in-budget solvability if at least one correct response terminates:
Qπ,L (p) = 1[∃i : ri = 1 ∧ fi = stop] .

(2)

Dependence on the target policy. We first hold the problem set and rollout budget fixed and
vary only the policy. We use the 15,470 problems that GPT-5-nano does not solve in every rollout,
discarding those it has already mastered, and evaluate them also with gpt-oss-120b using 8
rollouts per problem and 32K response limit. Changing only the policy causes nearly one third of
the problems (32.3%) to move between the zero-success, Goldilocks, and fully solved regions. Thus
relative to a target policy, the same problem can provide different learning signal.
Dependence on the rollout budget. We next hold the policy and evaluation set fixed and vary
only the maximum response length. Increasing the rollout budget of gpt-oss-120b from 16K
to 64K reduces the fraction of hard problems from 33.1% to 10.6% and raises the average accuracy
on the 8 math benchmarks from 61.2% to 78.9% (Figure 2b). Thus, the same problems appear
substantially harder under a smaller rollout budget, even though the policy is unchanged. Therefore,
a truncated response does not establish that the policy lacks the reasoning capability required by the
problem. It only establishes that no complete solution was produced within budget. We therefore
treat a problem as providing a clean in-budget group-relative learning signal only when:

V ERIFIED H ARD PROBLEMS SPECIFY FRONTIER CAPABILITIES

Hard problems play a paradoxical role in RLVR. Problems with empirical success rate zero provide
no direct group-relative advantage signal. Yet, these problems may still reveal reasoning capabilities
that the target policy lacks. We argue that a verified solution trace exposing the essential reasoning
operations together with how those operations are composed reveals the capability the target policy
lack. We refer to this structure as the problem’s reasoning blueprint. It provides a specification of capabilities at the frontier of the current policy. We define a verified hard problem as a solvable/correct
problem for which r̄π,L (p) = 0 and all sampled responses terminate normally:
r̄π,L (p) = 0

∀i ∈ {1, . . . , N }.

(4)

This termination requirement ensures that the observed failures reflect a reasoning limitation rather
than an artifact of the rollout budget.
Extracting reasoning blueprints. We study 98 math hard seed problems randomly selected from
the open corpus. For each seed, we obtain a verified solution and reasoning trace from a pool of
frontier models. We use a pool of teacher ( Gemini 3 Flash, Claude Opus 4.6, DeepSeek V4 Pro,
GPT 5.2 and Kimi K2.6) as we found with one attempt per model, the strongest individual teacher
solved only (59/98) seeds. From each trace, we then extract the essential reasoning operations and
how they are composed (i.e. we extract their reasoning blueprint).
Controlled in-context experiment. For each seed, we generate a simpler subproblem that instantiates some of the operations in the reasoning blueprint and provide its verified solution as a single
in-context example to the target policy. We use an LLM as a judge to verify the subproblem does
not explicitly reveal the solution of the original problem. For simplicity, and to avoid best-of-K selection effects, we use only the first generated subproblem. After this intervention, the policy solves
at least one rollout for 78/98 seeds (79.6%), while mean accuracy on the original hard problems increases from 0% to 46.9%. These results suggest that hard problems provide no direct RLVR signal
while still serving as useful specifications of missing reasoning capabilities.
Are blueprints consistent across frontier models? Among the 43 seeds with at least two verified
blueprints, we find 90.7% produce the same reasoning blueprint or a compatible variant. This suggests that the blueprint primarily reflects the structure of the problem rather than the idiosyncrasies
of a particular frontier model.
Observation. Hard problems are not merely training examples; they can be specifications of capabilities at the frontier of the current policy. We call a verified problem a capability-frontier seed
when the target policy cannot solve it reliably and its solution specifies a reasoning capability, or
composition of capabilities, beyond the policy’s current competence. The in-context experiment
shows one of the many actionable insights: a simpler, subproblem requiring a capability observed in
the blueprint of the original problems solution can substantially improves the target policy’s success
on the hard problem.

Seed synthesis preserves techniques but often makes shallow variants. We study this behavior

and dependencies in the seed blueprint, and whether the problem is substantively new rather than a
paraphrase, contextual rewrite, or numerical substitution. Figure 4 shows that seed synthesis often
retains recognizable reasoning techniques, particularly in math, but frequently does so through a
shallow variation of the original problem rather than a substantively new instantiation .

turn a shallow variant into a substantively new problem, nor produce a candidate for a seed that

F.1

B LUEPRINT P RESERVATION AND P ROBLEM N OVELTY

The reported study uses same-seed candidate pairs: one bottom-up MagicBeanStalk candidate and
one direct seed-synthesis candidate. The two candidates are selected in the observed Goldilocks
band and matched on the recorded target-policy correct-rollout count. The reported samples contain
300 math pairs and 100 finance pairs. A fixed reasoning blueprint is first extracted from the seed
problem, its verified solution, and its reasoning trace. Candidate scoring by GPT-5.6 is then symmetric and method-label blinded. The judge receives the seed, the fixed blueprint, and one generated
problem with its reference solution and answer, but no method name. The two methods are scored
in separate calls under the same rubric. The synthetic MBS tree is not exposed during candidate
scoring.
Reasoning preservation (1–5).
1. The required reasoning is different; any overlap is only topical or surface-level.
2. One minor or peripheral component overlaps, but the main reasoning is not preserved.
3. A meaningful subset of the core operations is required, with an important operation or dependency omitted or changed.
4. Most core operations and their principal dependencies are required; additional reasoning is allowed.
5. The same core operations and their composition are clearly required by the generated problem.
Problem novelty (1–5).
1. The candidate is effectively a duplicate or paraphrase of the seed.
2. The candidate mainly substitutes numbers, names, entities, documents, line items, or superficial
context.
3. The candidate is a meaningful variation of substantially the same setup.
4. The candidate is a substantively new setup that still instantiates the seed reasoning.
5. The candidate is a clearly new construction or composition that cannot be recovered through
superficial substitutions.
The scores are assigned independently. Novelty without blueprint preservation does not count as
successful transfer. The joint criterion reported in the paper is met only when both reasoning preservation and problem novelty are at least 4 for the same candidate.
